# Section 22.3: Data Mining

## Overview

The term data mining refers loosely to the process of semiautomatically analyzing large databases to find useful patterns. Like knowledge discovery in artificial intelligence (also called machine learning) or statistical analysis, data mining attempts to discover rules and patterns from data.

**Key Difference**: Data mining differs from machine learning and statistics in that it deals with large volumes of data, stored primarily on disk. That is, data mining deals with "knowledge discovery in databases."

### Types of Knowledge Discovered

Some types of knowledge discovered from a database can be represented by:
- **Rules**: Example: "Young women with annual incomes greater than $50,000 are the most likely people to buy small sports cars"
  - Such rules are not universally true
  - Have degrees of "support" and "confidence"
- **Equations**: Relating different variables to each other
- **Other mechanisms**: For predicting outcomes when values of some variables are known

### Manual Component

Usually there is a manual component to data mining:
- **Preprocessing**: Data to a form acceptable to the algorithms
- **Postprocessing**: Discovered patterns to find novel ones that could be useful
- **Pattern selection**: More than one type of pattern can be discovered; manual interaction may be needed to pick useful types

For this reason, data mining is really a semiautomatic process in real life. However, the description concentrates on the automatic aspect of mining.

## 22.3.1 Applications of Data Mining

The discovered knowledge has numerous applications.

### Prediction Applications

The most widely used applications require some sort of prediction:

**Credit Risk Prediction**:
- When a person applies for a credit card, the company wants to predict if the person is a good credit risk
- Prediction based on known attributes: age, income, debts, past debt repayment history
- Rules derived from same attributes of past and current credit card holders, along with their observed behavior (e.g., whether they defaulted)

**Other Prediction Types**:
- Predicting which customers may switch to a competitor (these customers may be offered special discounts)
- Predicting which people are likely to respond to promotional mail ("junk mail")
- Predicting what types of phone calling card usage are likely to be fraudulent

### Association Applications

Another class of applications looks for associations:

**Book Associations**:
- Books that tend to be bought together
- If a customer buys a book, an online bookstore may suggest associated books
- Example: Someone who bought "Database System Concepts" is likely to buy "Operating System Concepts"

**Product Associations**:
- If a person buys a camera, the system may suggest accessories that tend to be bought along with cameras
- A good salesperson is aware of such patterns and exploits them to make additional sales
- The challenge is to automate the process

**Store Layout**:
- A grocery shop may place bread close to milk (often bought together) to help shoppers finish faster
- Or place them at opposite ends with other associated items in between to tempt people to buy those items
- A shop that offers discounts on one associated item may not offer a discount on the other (customer will probably buy it anyway)

**Causation Discovery**:
- Other types of associations may lead to discovery of causation
- Example: Discovery of unexpected associations between a newly introduced medicine and cardiac problems led to finding that the medicine may cause cardiac problems in some people
- The medicine was then withdrawn from the market

### Descriptive Patterns

Associations are an example of descriptive patterns. Clusters are another example:

**Disease Clusters**:
- Example: Over a century ago, a cluster of typhoid cases was found around a well
- This led to the discovery that the water in the well was contaminated and was spreading typhoid
- Detection of clusters of disease remains important even today

## 22.3.2 Classification

As mentioned in Section 22.3.1, prediction is one of the most important types of data mining.

### Classification Problem

Abstractly, the classification problem is:
- Given that items belong to one of several classes
- Given past instances (called training instances) of items along with the classes to which they belong
- The problem is to predict the class to which a new item belongs
- The class of the new instance is not known, so other attributes of the instance must be used to predict the class

### Classification by Rules

Classification can be done by finding rules that partition the given data into disjoint groups.

**Example: Credit Card Application**:
- A credit-card company wants to decide whether to give a credit card to an applicant
- The company has information: age, educational background, annual income, current debts
- Some information is relevant to credit worthiness, some may not be
- The company assigns a creditworthiness level (excellent, good, average, or bad) to each sample set of current customers according to payment history
- The company attempts to find rules that classify current customers into these levels based on information other than payment history (unavailable for new customers)

**Example Rules**:
- ∀person P, P.degree = masters and P.income > 75,000 ⇒ P.credit = excellent
- ∀person P, P.degree = bachelors or (P.income ≥ 25,000 and P.income ≤ 75,000) ⇒ P.credit = good

Similar rules would be present for other credit worthiness levels (average and bad).

### Training Set

The process of building a classifier starts from a sample of data, called a training set:
- For each tuple in the training set, the class to which the tuple belongs is already known
- Example: Training set for a credit-card application may be existing customers, with credit worthiness determined from payment history
- The actual data (population) may consist of all people, including those who are not existing customers

## 22.3.2.1 Decision Tree Classifiers

The decision tree classifier is a widely used technique for classification.

### Structure

- Each leaf node has an associated class
- Each internal node has a predicate (or more generally, a function) associated with it

### Classification Process

To classify a new instance:
1. Start at the root
2. Traverse the tree to reach a leaf
3. At each internal node, evaluate the predicate (or function) on the data instance to find which child to go to
4. Continue until reaching a leaf node
5. The class at the leaf is the predicted class

**Example**: If degree level is masters and income is 40K:
- Start from root, follow edge labeled "masters"
- From there, follow edge labeled "25K to 75K"
- Reach leaf with class "good"
- Predict credit risk is "good"

### Building Decision Tree Classifiers

The question is how to build a decision tree classifier given a set of training instances.

**Greedy Algorithm Approach**:
- Works recursively, starting at the root and building the tree downward
- Initially there is only one node (the root), and all training instances are associated with that node
- At each node:
  - If all or "almost all" training instances belong to the same class, the node becomes a leaf node associated with that class
  - Otherwise, a partitioning attribute and partitioning conditions must be selected to create child nodes
  - The data associated with each child node is the set of training instances that satisfy the partitioning condition for that child node

**Example**:
- Attribute degree is chosen, four children created (one for each value: none, bachelors, masters, doctorate)
- At the node corresponding to masters, attribute income is chosen
- Range of values partitioned into intervals: 0-25,000, 25,000-50,000, 50,000-75,000, over 75,000
- Optimization: Since class for 25,000-50,000 and 50,000-75,000 is the same, ranges merged into single range 25,000-75,000

### Best Splits

**Intuition**:
- By choosing a sequence of partitioning attributes, we start with the set of all training instances ("impure" - contains instances from many classes)
- End up with leaves which are "pure" (at each leaf, all training instances belong to only one class)

**Measuring Purity**:
To judge the benefit of picking a particular attribute and condition, measure the purity of the data at the children resulting from partitioning. The attribute and condition that result in maximum purity are chosen.

**Gini Measure**:
- Gini(S) = 1 - Σ(pi²) for i = 1 to k
- Where k is the number of classes, and pi is the fraction of instances in class i
- When all instances are in a single class, Gini = 0
- Reaches maximum (1 - 1/k) if each class has the same number of instances

**Entropy Measure**:
- Entropy(S) = -Σ(pi log₂ pi) for i = 1 to k
- Entropy = 0 if all instances are in a single class
- Reaches maximum when each class has the same number of instances
- Derives from information theory

**Purity of Split Sets**:
- When set S is split into sets Si (i = 1, 2, ..., r):
- Purity(S1, S2, ..., Sr) = Σ(|Si|/|S| × purity(Si))
- This is the weighted average of the purity of the sets Si
- Can be used with both Gini and entropy measures

**Information Gain**:
- Information-gain(S, {S1, S2, ..., Sr}) = purity(S) - purity(S1, S2, ..., Sr)

**Information Content**:
- Information-content(S, {S1, S2, ..., Sr}) = -Σ(|Si|/|S| × log₂(|Si|/|S|))
- Splits into fewer sets are preferable (simpler, more meaningful decision trees)
- The number of elements in each set may also be taken into account

**Best Split Definition**:
- The best split for an attribute is the one that gives the maximum information gain ratio:
  - Information-gain / Information-content

### Finding Best Splits

How to split an attribute depends on the type of the attribute:

**Continuous-Valued Attributes**:
- Values can be ordered in a fashion meaningful to classification (e.g., age, income)
- Usually numbers (integers/reals) are treated as continuous valued
- For simplicity, consider only binary splits (splits that result in two children)
- To find best binary split:
  1. Sort the attribute values in the training instances
  2. Compute information gain obtained by splitting at each value
  3. Example: If values are 1, 10, 15, 25, split points considered are 1, 10, 15
  4. Values ≤ split point form one partition, rest form the other
  5. Best binary split is the one giving maximum information gain

**Categorical Attributes**:
- Have no meaningful order (e.g., department names, country names)
- Usually character string attributes are treated as categorical
- Can have multiway split, with a child for each value of the attribute
- Works fine for attributes with few distinct values (e.g., degree, gender)
- If attribute has many distinct values (e.g., department names in large company), creating a child for each value is not a good idea
- In such cases, combine multiple values into each child to create smaller number of children

### Decision-Tree Construction Algorithm

**Main Idea**:
- Evaluate different attributes and different partitioning conditions
- Pick the attribute and partitioning condition that results in maximum information gain ratio
- Same procedure works recursively on each of the sets resulting from the split

**Recursive Process**:
- If data can be perfectly classified, recursion stops when purity of a set is 0
- Often data are noisy, or a set may be so small that further partitioning is not statistically justified
- In this case, recursion stops when purity is "sufficiently high"
- The class of resulting leaf is defined as the class of the majority of elements of the set
- Different branches of the tree can grow to different levels

**Pseudocode**:
```
procedure GrowTree(S)
  Partition(S);

procedure Partition(S)
  if (purity(S) > δp or |S| < δs) then
    return;
  for each attribute A
    evaluate splits on attribute A;
  Use best split found (across all attributes) to partition S into S1, S2, ..., Sr;
  for i = 1, 2, ..., r
    Partition(Si);
```

**Parameters**:
- δp and δs define cutoffs for purity and size
- System may give default values that can be overridden by users

### Algorithm Variations

There are a wide variety of decision tree construction algorithms:

**Large Data Sets**:
- Partitioning may be expensive (involves repeated copying)
- Several algorithms developed to minimize I/O and computation cost when training data are larger than available memory

**Pruning**:
- Several algorithms prune subtrees of the generated decision tree to reduce overfitting
- A subtree is overfitted if it has been so highly tuned to the specifics of the training data that it makes many classification errors on other data
- A subtree is pruned by replacing it with a leaf node
- Different pruning heuristics exist
- One heuristic: Use part of training data to build tree and another part to test it
- Prune a subtree if misclassification on test instances would be reduced if subtree were replaced by a leaf node

**Classification Rules**:
- Can generate classification rules from a decision tree
- For each leaf, generate a rule:
  - Left-hand side: conjunction of all split conditions on the path to the leaf
  - Class: class of the majority of training instances at the leaf
- Example: degree = masters and income > 75,000 ⇒ excellent

## 22.3.2.2 Other Types of Classifiers

There are several types of classifiers other than decision tree classifiers.

### Neural Net Classifiers

- Use training data to train artificial neural nets
- Large body of literature on neural nets
- Not considered further here

### Bayesian Classifiers

**Approach**:
- Find the distribution of attribute values for each class in the training data
- When given a new instance d, use distribution information to estimate, for each class cj, the probability that instance d belongs to class cj, denoted by p(cj|d)
- The class with maximum probability becomes the predicted class for instance d

**Bayes' Theorem**:
- p(cj|d) = p(d|cj) × p(cj) / p(d)
- Where:
  - p(d|cj) is the probability of generating instance d given class cj
  - p(cj) is the probability of occurrence of class cj
  - p(d) is the probability of instance d occurring
- p(d) can be ignored since it's the same for all classes
- p(cj) is simply the fraction of training instances that belong to class cj

**Naive Bayesian Classifiers**:
- Finding p(d|cj) exactly is difficult (requires complete distribution of instances of cj)
- To simplify, assume attributes have independent distributions
- Estimate: p(d|cj) = p(d1|cj) × p(d2|cj) × ... × p(dn|cj)
- The probability of instance d occurring is the product of the probability of occurrence of each attribute value di of d, given the class is cj

**Computing Probabilities**:
- The probabilities p(di|cj) derive from the distribution of values for each attribute i, for each class cj
- Distribution computed from training instances that belong to each class cj
- Usually approximated by a histogram
- Example: Divide range of values of attribute i into equal intervals, store fraction of instances of class cj that fall in each interval
- Given a value di for attribute i, p(di|cj) is the fraction of instances belonging to class cj that fall in the interval to which di belongs

**Advantage**:
- Can classify instances with unknown and null attribute values
- Unknown or null attributes are just omitted from the probability computation
- In contrast, decision tree classifiers cannot meaningfully handle situations where an instance to be classified has a null value for a partitioning attribute used to traverse further down the decision tree

## 22.3.2.3 Regression

Regression deals with the prediction of a value, rather than a class.

**Problem**:
- Given values for a set of variables X1, X2, ..., Xn
- Wish to predict the value of a variable Y
- Example: Treat level of education as a number and income as another number, predict likelihood of default (percentage chance or amount involved)

**Linear Regression**:
- Infer coefficients a0, a1, a2, ..., an such that:
  Y = a0 + a1 × X1 + a2 × X2 + ... + an × Xn
- Finding such a linear polynomial is called linear regression

**Curve Fitting**:
- In general, wish to find a curve (defined by a polynomial or other formula) that fits the data
- Also called curve fitting
- The fit may only be approximate due to noise in data or because the relationship is not exactly a polynomial
- Regression aims to find coefficients that give the best possible fit
- Standard techniques in statistics exist for finding regression coefficients

## 22.3.3 Association Rules

Retail shops are often interested in associations between different items that people buy.

### Examples of Associations

- Someone who buys bread is quite likely also to buy milk
- A person who bought "Database System Concepts" is quite likely to buy "Operating System Concepts"

### Uses of Association Information

**Online Suggestions**:
- When a customer buys a particular book, an online shop may suggest associated books
- If a person buys a camera, the system may suggest accessories that tend to be bought along with cameras

**Store Layout**:
- A grocery shop may place bread close to milk (often bought together) to help shoppers finish faster
- Or place them at opposite ends with other associated items in between to tempt people to buy those items as shoppers walk from one end to the other

**Discount Strategy**:
- A shop that offers discounts on one associated item may not offer a discount on the other (customer will probably buy it anyway)

### Association Rules

**Example**: bread ⇒ milk

- In the context of grocery-store purchases, the rule says that customers who buy bread also tend to buy milk with a high probability
- An association rule must have an associated population
- The population consists of a set of instances

**Population Examples**:
- Grocery-store: Population may consist of all grocery store purchases; each purchase is an instance
- Bookstore: Population may consist of all people who made purchases, regardless of when; each customer is an instance
- Analyst decides what constitutes an instance (e.g., single purchases vs. multiple visits by same customer)

### Support and Confidence

Rules have an associated support and confidence, defined in the context of the population:

**Support**:
- Measure of what fraction of the population satisfies both the antecedent and the consequent of the rule
- Example: If only 0.001% of all purchases include milk and screwdrivers, support for "milk ⇒ screwdrivers" is low
- Rule may not be statistically significant (perhaps only single purchase included both)
- Businesses usually not interested in rules with low support (involve few customers, not worth bothering about)
- If 50% of all purchases involve milk and bread, support for rules involving bread and milk is relatively high
- Exactly what minimum degree of support is considered desirable depends on the application

**Confidence**:
- Measure of how often the consequent is true when the antecedent is true
- Example: "bread ⇒ milk" has confidence of 80% if 80% of purchases that include bread also include milk
- A rule with low confidence is not meaningful
- In business applications, rules usually have confidences significantly less than 100%
- In other domains (e.g., physics), rules may have high confidences
- Note: Confidence of "bread ⇒ milk" may be very different from confidence of "milk ⇒ bread", although both have the same support

### Finding Association Rules

To discover association rules of the form i1, i2, ..., in ⇒ i0:

**Step 1: Find Large Itemsets**
- First find sets of items with sufficient support, called large itemsets
- Find sets of items that are included in a sufficiently large number of instances

**Step 2: Output Rules**
- For each large itemset, output all rules with sufficient confidence that involve all and only the elements of the set
- For each large itemset S, output a rule S - s ⇒ s for every subset s ⊂ S, provided S - s ⇒ s has sufficient confidence
- Confidence of the rule is given by support of s divided by support of S

**Generating Large Itemsets**:
- If number of possible sets of items is small, a single pass over data suffices to detect support for all sets
- Maintain a count (initialized to 0) for each set of items
- When a purchase record is fetched, increment count for each set of items such that all items in the set are contained in the purchase
- Example: If purchase included items a, b, c, increment counts for {a}, {b}, {c}, {a,b}, {b,c}, {a,c}, {a,b,c}
- Sets with sufficiently high count at end of pass correspond to items with high degree of association

**Problem with Exponential Growth**:
- Number of sets grows exponentially, making procedure infeasible if number of items is large
- Luckily, almost all sets would normally have very low support
- Optimizations developed to eliminate most such sets from consideration
- These techniques use multiple passes on database, considering only some sets in each pass

**A Priori Technique**:
- Only sets with single items considered in first pass
- Sets with two items considered in second pass, and so on
- At end of a pass, all sets with sufficient support output as large itemsets
- Sets found to have too little support at end of pass are eliminated
- Once a set is eliminated, none of its supersets needs to be considered
- In pass i, need to count only supports for sets of size i such that all subsets of the set have been found to have sufficiently high support
- Test all subsets of size i-1 to ensure this property
- At end of some pass i, if no set of size i has sufficient support, don't need to consider any set of size i+1
- Computation then terminates

## 22.3.4 Other Types of Associations

### Correlations

Using plain association rules has several shortcomings:
- Many associations are not very interesting since they can be predicted
- Example: If many people buy cereal and many people buy bread, can predict fairly large number of people would buy both, even if no connection between purchases
- What's interesting is deviation from expected co-occurrence
- In statistical terms, look for correlations between items
- Correlations can be:
  - Positive: co-occurrence is higher than would have been expected
  - Negative: items co-occur less frequently than predicted

### Sequence Associations

Another important class is sequence associations (or correlations):
- Time-series data (e.g., stock prices on sequence of days) form example of sequence data
- Stock-market analysts want to find associations among stock-market price sequences
- Example: "Whenever bond rates go up, the stock prices go down within 2 days"
- Discovering such associations between sequences can help make intelligent investment decisions

### Temporal Pattern Deviations

Deviations from temporal patterns are often interesting:
- Example: If company has been growing at steady rate each year, deviation from usual growth rate is surprising
- If sales of winter clothes go down in summer, not surprising (can predict from past years)
- Deviation that could not have been predicted from past experience is considered interesting
- Mining techniques can find deviations from what would have been expected on basis of past temporal/sequential patterns

## 22.3.5 Clustering

Intuitively, clustering refers to the problem of finding clusters of points in the given data.

### Formalization

The problem of clustering can be formalized from distance metrics in several ways:

**Approach 1**: Group points into k sets (for given k) so that the average distance of points from the centroid of their assigned cluster is minimized

**Approach 2**: Group points so that the average distance between every pair of points in each cluster is minimized

**Centroid Definition**: The centroid of a set of points is a point whose coordinate on each dimension is the average of the coordinates of all points of that set on that dimension

**Intuition**: All definitions aim to group similar points together in a single set

### Hierarchical Clustering

Another type of clustering appears in classification systems in biology:
- Such classification systems do not attempt to predict classes, rather they attempt to cluster related items together
- Example: Leopards and humans clustered under class mammalia, crocodiles and snakes under reptilia
- Both mammalia and reptilia come under common class chordata
- Clustering of mammalia has further subclusters (e.g., carnivora, primates)
- This is hierarchical clustering

**Applications**:
- Hierarchical clustering useful in other domains (e.g., clustering documents)
- Internet directory systems (such as Yahoo's) cluster related documents in hierarchical fashion

**Algorithm Types**:
- **Agglomerative clustering algorithms**: Start by building small clusters, then create higher levels
- **Divisive clustering algorithms**: First create higher levels of hierarchical clustering, then refine each resulting cluster into lower level clusters

### Scalable Clustering Algorithms

The statistics community has studied clustering extensively. Database research has provided scalable clustering algorithms that can cluster very large data sets (that may not fit in memory).

**Birch Clustering Algorithm**:
- Data points are inserted into a multidimensional tree structure (based on R-trees)
- Guided to appropriate leaf nodes based on nearness to representative points in internal nodes of tree
- Nearby points are clustered together in leaf nodes, and summarized if there are more points than fit in memory
- Some postprocessing after insertion of all points gives desired overall clustering

### Collaborative Filtering

An interesting application of clustering is to predict what new movies (or books, or music) a person is likely to be interested in, based on:
1. The person's past preferences in movies
2. Other people with similar past preferences
3. The preferences of such people for new movies

**Approach**:
- To find people with similar past preferences, create clusters of people based on their preferences for movies
- Accuracy of clustering can be improved by previously clustering movies by their similarity
- Even if people have not seen the same movies, if they have seen similar movies they would be clustered together
- Repeat clustering: alternately cluster people, then movies, then people, etc. until equilibrium
- Given a new user, find cluster of users most similar to that user based on user's preferences for movies already seen
- Predict movies in movie clusters that are popular with that user's cluster as likely to be interesting to the new user
- This is an instance of collaborative filtering, where users collaborate in the task of filtering information to find information of interest

## 22.3.6 Other Types of Mining

### Text Mining

Text mining applies data mining techniques to textual documents:
- Tools that form clusters on pages that a user has visited
- Helps users when they browse history of their browsing to find pages visited earlier
- Distance between pages can be based on common words in the pages
- Another application: classify pages into a Web directory automatically according to their similarity with other pages

### Data Visualization

Data-visualization systems help users to examine large volumes of data and detect patterns visually:

**Visual Displays**:
- Maps, charts, and other graphical representations allow data to be presented compactly to users
- A single graphical screen can encode as much information as a far larger number of text screens

**Examples**:
- To find out whether production problems at plants are correlated to locations, problem locations can be encoded in a special color (e.g., red) on a map
- User can quickly discover locations where problems are occurring
- User may form hypotheses about why problems are occurring in those locations and verify hypotheses quantitatively against database
- Information about values can be encoded as a color and displayed with as little as one pixel of screen area
- To detect associations between pairs of items, use two-dimensional pixel matrix with each row and column representing an item
- Percentage of transactions that buy both items can be encoded by color intensity of pixel
- Items with high association show up as bright pixels against darker background

**Key Point**:
- Data visualization systems do not automatically detect patterns
- They provide system support for users to detect patterns
- Since humans are very good at detecting visual patterns, data visualization is an important component of data mining
