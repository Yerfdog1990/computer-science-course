## 1. Introduction — The Historical Drivers

### 1.1 The Pace of Change

> **"The pace at which computer systems change was, is, and continues to be overwhelming."**

- **From 1945** (when the modern computer era began) **until about 1985**, computers were **large and expensive**. Moreover, for **lack of a way to connect them**, these computers **operated independently** from one another.

### 1.2 Two Technological Advances (Mid-1980s Onward)

**Advance 1 — Powerful Microprocessors**
- Initially **8-bit machines**, but soon **16-, 32-, and 64-bit CPUs** became common.
- **With multicore CPUs**, we now are **refacing the challenge** of adapting and developing programs to **exploit parallelism**.
- **Current generation machines** have the computing power of the **mainframes deployed 30–40 years ago**, but for **1/1000th of the price or less.**

**Advance 2 — High-Speed Computer Networks**
- **Local-area networks (LANs):** allow thousands of machines within a building/campus to connect such that small amounts of information transfer in **a few microseconds**; larger amounts move at **rates of billions of bits per second (bps)**.
- **Wide-area networks (WANs):** allow hundreds of millions of machines all over the earth to connect at speeds varying from **tens of thousands to hundreds of millions of bps**, and sometimes even faster.

### 1.3 Miniaturization

- Parallel to increasingly powerful, networked machines, we've witnessed **miniaturization of computer systems**, with perhaps the **smartphone** as the most impressive outcome — packed with sensors, memory, and a powerful CPU, these are **"nothing less than full-fledged computers"** with networking capabilities.
- **Plug computers** and other **"nanocomputers"** are also finding their way to market — often **the size of a power adapter**, plugged directly into an outlet, offering **near-desktop performance.**

### 1.4 The Resulting Landscape

> The result of these technologies is that it is now **not only feasible, but easy**, to put together a computing system composed of **many networked computers**, large or small.

- These computers are generally **geographically dispersed**, which is why they form a **distributed system.**
- **Size varies:** from a **handful of devices** to **millions of computers.**
- **Interconnection network:** wired, wireless, or a **combination of both.**
- **Distributed systems are often highly dynamic:** computers can **join and leave**, with the **topology and performance** of the underlying network **almost continuously changing.**

## 2. What Is a Distributed System?

### 2.1 No Satisfactory Consensus Definition Exists

> **"Various definitions of distributed systems have been given in the literature, none of them satisfactory, and none of them in agreement with any of the others."**

### 2.2 The Paper's Working Definition

> **A distributed system is a collection of autonomous computing elements that appears to its users as a single coherent system.**

This definition refers to **two characteristic features**:

1. **A collection of computing elements**, each able to behave **independently** of each other.
2. **Users (people or applications) believe they are dealing with a single system** — meaning the autonomous nodes need to **collaborate.**

> *"How to establish this collaboration lies at the heart of developing distributed systems."*

### 2.3 No Assumptions About Node Type or Interconnection

- We are **not making any assumptions** concerning the **type of nodes** — even within a single system, they could range from **high-performance mainframe computers** to **small devices in sensor networks.**
- Likewise, **no assumptions** are made concerning **how nodes are interconnected.**

## 3. Characteristic 1 — Collection of Autonomous Computing Elements

### 3.1 Independence and the "Node" Concept

- Modern distributed systems can consist of **all kinds of nodes**, from very big high-performance computers to small plug computers or **even smaller devices.**
- **Fundamental principle:** nodes can **act independently** from each other — although *"it should be obvious that if they ignore each other, then there is no use in putting them into the same distributed system."*
- **In practice:** nodes are programmed to achieve **common goals**, realized by **exchanging messages** with each other. A node **reacts to incoming messages**, which are then processed and, in turn, lead to **further communication** through message passing.

### 3.2 No Global Clock

> An important observation is that, as a consequence of dealing with independent nodes, **each one will have its own notion of time.** We **cannot assume** there is something like a **global clock.**

- This lack of a common reference of time leads to **fundamental questions** regarding **synchronization and coordination** within a distributed system.

### 3.3 Managing Group Membership

- Since we're dealing with a **collection of nodes**, we may need to **manage the membership and organization** of that collection — i.e., **register which nodes may or may not belong** to the system, and provide each member with a list of nodes it can directly communicate with.

#### Open Groups vs. Closed Groups

| Group Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Open group** | Any node is **allowed to join** the distributed system, effectively meaning it can send messages to **any** other node in the system. |
| **Closed group** | Only the **members** of that group can communicate with each other; a **separate mechanism** is needed to let a node join or leave. |

#### Why Admission Control Is Difficult

1. A **mechanism is needed to authenticate a node** — if not properly designed, this can easily create a **scalability bottleneck.**
2. Each node must, in principle, **check** if it's indeed communicating with another group member and **not, for example, an intruder** aiming to create havoc.
3. Since a member can easily communicate with **nonmembers**, if **confidentiality** is an issue, we may be **facing trust issues.**

### 3.4 Overlay Networks

> Practice shows that a distributed system is often organized as an **overlay network.**

- A **node** is typically a **software process** equipped with a **list of other processes** it can directly send messages to (a neighbor may also need to be **first looked up**).
- **Message passing** is done through **TCP/IP or UDP channels**, but higher-level facilities may also be available.

**Two basic types of overlay networks:**

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Structured overlay** | Each node has a **well-defined set of neighbors** with whom it can communicate — e.g., nodes organized in a **tree** or **logical ring.** |
| **Unstructured overlay** | Each node has a number of references to **randomly selected** other nodes. |

- **A well-known class of overlays:** peer-to-peer (P2P) networks.
- **Requirement:** an overlay network should, in principle, always be **connected** — meaning between any two nodes there's always a **communication path** allowing them to route messages to each other.
- **Organizing nodes requires special effort** — it's *"sometimes one of the more intricate parts of distributed-systems management."*

## 4. Characteristic 2 — Single Coherent System

### 4.1 "Single-System View" vs. "Appears to Be Coherent"

- Some researchers have gone so far as to argue for a **"single-system view"** — meaning an end user should not even notice that processes, data, and control are dispersed across a computer network.
- **This is often asking too much.** The paper's definition instead opts for the **weaker** requirement: that the system **appears to be coherent.**

> **Roughly speaking, a distributed system is coherent if it behaves according to the expectations of its users.** More specifically, in a single coherent system, the collection of nodes as a whole **operates the same, no matter where, when, and how** interaction between a user and the system takes place.

### 4.2 What Coherence Requires — Distribution Transparency

- An end user should **not be able to tell** exactly on which computer a process is currently executing, or that part of a task was spawned off to another process executing elsewhere.
- **Where data is stored** should be of no concern, nor should it matter that the system may be **replicating data** to enhance performance.
- This is called **distribution transparency** — an important design goal (detailed in §8).
- **Analogy:** it's akin to the approach in many **Unix-like operating systems**, where resources are accessed through a **unifying file-system interface**, effectively hiding the differences between files, storage devices, main memory, and networks.

### 4.3 The Inevitable Trade-off — Partial Failures

> Striving for a single coherent system introduces an important trade-off. As we **cannot ignore** the fact that a distributed system consists of multiple, networked nodes, it is **inevitable that at any time only a part of the system fails.**

- Unexpected behavior — where some applications continue to execute successfully while others come to a **grinding halt** — is a **reality that needs to be dealt with.**
- Although partial failures are inherent to any complex system, in distributed systems **they are particularly difficult to hide.**

### 4.4 Lamport's Famous Quote

> Turing-award winner **Leslie Lamport** described a distributed system as: *"[...] one in which the failure of a computer you did not even know existed can render your own computer unusable."*

## 5. Middleware and Distributed Systems

### 5.1 The Layered Organization

- To assist development, distributed systems are often organized to have a **separate layer of software** logically placed **on top of** the respective operating systems of the computers that are part of the system — this is known as **middleware.**

```
        Same interface everywhere
   Computer 1     Computer 2     Computer 3     Computer 4
   [Appl. A]      [Application B: spans Computers 2 & 3]      [Appl. C]
   ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │           Distributed-system layer (middleware)        │
   └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
   [Local OS 1]   [Local OS 2]   [Local OS 3]   [Local OS 4]
                        Network
```

- **Application B is distributed across computers 2 and 3.** Each application is offered the **same interface.**
- Middleware provides the means for components of a **single distributed application** to communicate, **and** lets **different applications** communicate. At the same time, it **hides**, as best/reasonably as possible, the **differences in hardware and operating systems** from each application.

### 5.2 Middleware as an "Operating System" for the Network

> In a sense, **middleware is the same to a distributed system as what an operating system is to a computer**: a manager of resources offering applications the means to efficiently share and deploy those resources across a network.

**Services middleware offers, similar to an OS:**
- Facilities for **interapplication communication.**
- **Security services.**
- **Accounting services.**
- **Masking of and recovery from failures.**

- **Main difference from OS equivalents:** middleware services are offered in a **networked environment.**
- Middleware can also be viewed as a **"container of commonly used components and functions"** that no longer have to be implemented separately by applications.

### 5.3 Typical Middleware Services

| Service | What It Does |
|---|---|
| **Communication (RPC)** | The **Remote Procedure Call** allows an application to invoke a function implemented and executed on a **remote computer** as if it were locally available. A developer specifies just the function header (in a special programming language); the RPC subsystem generates the code establishing remote invocations. |
| **Transactions** | Middleware offers special support for executing services distributed among several computers in an **all-or-nothing fashion**, called an **atomic transaction**. The developer specifies the remote services involved, and (via a standardized protocol) the middleware ensures every service is invoked, or none at all. |
| **Service composition** | Increasingly common to build new applications by **gluing together existing programs** — notably in **Web services**. Web-based middleware standardizes how Web services are accessed. Example: **mashups** — web pages combining/aggregating data from different sources (e.g., Google Maps enhanced with trip planners or real-time weather). |
| **Reliability** | Enhanced functions for building reliable distributed applications. Example: the **Horus toolkit**, which lets a developer build an application as a group of processes such that any message sent by one process is guaranteed to be received by **all or no** other process. |

## 6. Design Goals — Overview

> **Just because it is possible to build distributed systems does not necessarily mean that it is a good idea.**

There are **four important goals** that should be met to make building a distributed system worth the effort:

1. It should make **resources easily accessible.**
2. It should **hide** the fact that resources are distributed across a network.
3. It should be **open.**
4. It should be **scalable.**

---

## 7. Design Goal 1: Supporting Resource Sharing

### 7.1 The Core Goal

> An important goal of a distributed system is to make it **easy for users (and applications) to access and share remote resources.**

- Resources can be virtually anything: **peripherals, storage facilities, data, files, services, and networks**, to name just a few.

### 7.2 Reasons for Wanting to Share Resources

**Reason 1 — Economics**
- It's **cheaper** to have a single high-end reliable storage facility be **shared**, than to buy and maintain storage for each user separately.

**Reason 2 — Collaboration**
- Connecting users and resources makes it **easier to collaborate and exchange information**, as illustrated by the success of the **Internet** with its simple protocols for exchanging files, mail, documents, audio, and video.
- The Internet's connectivity has allowed **geographically widely dispersed groups** to work together via **groupware** (collaborative editing, teleconferencing, etc.) — as illustrated by multinational software-development companies that have **outsourced code production to Asia.**

**Reason 3 — Peer-to-Peer File Sharing (The Best Illustration)**
> Resource sharing in distributed systems is perhaps **best illustrated by the success of file-sharing peer-to-peer networks like BitTorrent.**

- These make it extremely simple for users to **share files across the Internet.**
- Often associated with **distribution of media files** (audio, video), but also used for **large amounts of data** — e.g., **software updates, backup services, and data synchronization across multiple servers.**

## 8. Design Goal 2: Making Distribution Transparent

### 8.1 The Core Goal

> An important goal is to **hide the fact that its processes and resources are physically distributed** across multiple computers, possibly separated by large distances — that is, to make distribution **transparent** (invisible) to end users and applications.

### 8.2 The Seven Types of Distribution Transparency (Table 1, from ISO)

| Transparency | Description |
|---|---|
| **Access** | Hide differences in **data representation** and how an object is accessed. |
| **Location** | Hide **where** an object is located. |
| **Relocation** | Hide that an object **may be moved to another location while in use.** |
| **Migration** | Hide that an object **may move to another location.** |
| **Replication** | Hide that an object is **replicated.** |
| **Concurrency** | Hide that an object may be **shared by several independent users.** |
| **Failure** | Hide the **failure and recovery** of an object. |

*(The term "object" here means either a process or a resource.)*

### 8.3 Access Transparency — Detail

- Deals with hiding differences in **data representation** and the way objects can be accessed.
- At a basic level, we want to hide differences in **machine architectures**, but more important is reaching **agreement on how data is represented** by different machines/OSes.
- Examples of access issues to hide: differences in **file-naming conventions**, differences in **file operations**, differences in **low-level communication.**

### 8.4 Location, Relocation, and Migration Transparency

- **Location transparency:** users **cannot tell where** an object is physically located. **Naming plays an important role** — achieved by assigning only **logical names** to resources (names that don't secretly encode location).
    - **Example:** the URL `http://www.distributed-systems.net/index.php` gives **no clue** about the actual location of the site's web server, nor whether `index.php` has always been there or was recently moved.
- **Relocation transparency:** the entire site may have been **moved** from one datacenter to another to make more efficient use of disk space — yet users shouldn't notice. **Increasingly important in cloud computing.**
- **Migration transparency:** offered when a distributed system supports the **mobility of processes and resources initiated by users**, without affecting ongoing communication/operations.
    - **Typical example:** communication between mobile phones — regardless of whether two people are actually moving, mobile phones let them **continue their conversation.**
    - Other examples: **online tracking/tracing of goods** during transport, **teleconferencing** using mobile-Internet-equipped devices.

### 8.5 Replication Transparency

> Replication plays an important role in distributed systems — resources may be replicated to **increase availability** or **improve performance** (placing a copy close to where it's accessed).

- Deals with hiding the fact that **several copies** of a resource exist, or that several processes operate in some form of **lockstep mode** so one can take over when another fails.
- **Requirement:** to hide replication, **all replicas must have the same name.** Consequently, a system supporting replication transparency should **generally also support location transparency**, since otherwise it would be impossible to refer to replicas at different locations.

### 8.6 Concurrency Transparency

- Resource sharing is often done **cooperatively** (e.g., communication channels), but there are also many examples of **competitive** sharing — e.g., two independent users each storing files on the **same** file server, or accessing the **same** tables in a shared database.
- **Requirement:** each user should **not notice** the other is making use of the same resource — this is **concurrency transparency.**
- **Key issue:** concurrent access to a shared resource must leave that resource in a **consistent state.**
    - Achieved through **locking mechanisms** (exclusive access, in turn).
    - A more refined mechanism: **transactions** — though these **may be difficult to implement** in a distributed system, notably when scalability is an issue.

### 8.7 Failure Transparency

> **Last, but certainly not least**, a distributed system should provide **failure transparency** — meaning a user/application **does not notice** that some piece of the system **fails to work properly**, and that the system subsequently (and automatically) **recovers** from that failure.

- **Masking failures is one of the HARDEST issues** in distributed systems, and is even **IMPOSSIBLE** when certain apparently realistic assumptions are made.
- **The main difficulty:** the **inability to distinguish** between a **dead process** and a **painfully slowly responding one.**
- **Example:** when contacting a busy web server, a browser will eventually **time out** and report the page is unavailable. At that point, the **user cannot tell** whether the server is actually down, or the network is badly congested.

### 8.8 The Degree of Distribution Transparency — When Full Transparency Is NOT a Good Idea

> Although distribution transparency is generally considered preferable, there are situations in which **attempting to blindly hide all distribution aspects from users is not a good idea.**

#### Example 1 — The Electronic Newspaper Across Time Zones
- Requesting your electronic newspaper to appear in your mailbox before 7 A.M. **local time**, as usual, while you're currently on **the other end of the world in a different time zone** — your morning paper will not be the morning paper you're used to.

#### Example 2 — The Speed of Light
- A wide-area distributed system connecting a process in San Francisco to a process in Amsterdam **cannot be expected to hide the fact that Mother Nature will not allow it** to send a message from one process to the other in **less than approximately 35ms.**
- **Practice shows it actually takes several hundred ms** using a computer network — signal transmission is limited not only by the speed of light, but also by **processing capacities and delays in intermediate switches.**

#### Example 3 — The Transparency/Performance Trade-off
- Many Internet applications **repeatedly try to contact a server** before finally giving up. Attempting to **mask a transient server failure** before trying another one may **slow down the system as a whole.**
- It might have been **better to give up earlier**, or at least let the user **cancel** the attempt.

#### Example 4 — Cross-Continent Replica Consistency
- If we need to guarantee that several replicas, located on **different continents**, must be **consistent all the time** — if one copy changes, that change must propagate to **all copies before allowing any other operation.**
- A single update operation may then take **seconds to complete** — something that **cannot be hidden** from users.

#### Example 5 — Location-Based Services
- As distributed systems expand to devices people carry around, and **location/context awareness** becomes increasingly important, it may be best to **actually EXPOSE distribution** rather than hide it.
- **Example:** location-based services on mobile phones — finding the nearest Chinese take-away, or checking if any friends are nearby.

### 8.9 Researchers Arguing AGAINST Full Transparency

- Several researchers argue that hiding distribution will only **further complicate** the development of distributed systems, precisely because **full distribution transparency can never be achieved.**
- **Waldo et al.** point out that attempting to hide distribution by means of **remote procedure calls** can lead to **poorly understood semantics** — a procedure call **DOES change** when executed over a **faulty communication link.**
- **Alternative approaches:** using **message-style communication** more explicitly, or explicitly **posting requests to, and getting results from,** remote machines — as is done in the Web when fetching pages.

#### Wams' Radical Position — The Copy-Before-Use Principle
- **Wams** states that **partial failures preclude relying on the successful execution of a remote service.** If such reliability cannot be guaranteed, it's **best to always perform only local executions**, leading to the **copy-before-use principle.**
- **Under this principle:** data can be accessed **only after** it has been transferred to the machine of the process wanting that data. **Modifying a data item should not be done** — instead, it can only be **updated to a new version.**
- Despite the many problems this raises, **Wams shows many existing applications can be retrofitted** to this alternative approach **without sacrificing functionality.**

### 8.10 Conclusion on Transparency

> Aiming for distribution transparency may be a nice goal, but it should be considered **together with other issues** such as **performance and comprehensibility.** **The price for achieving full transparency may be surprisingly high.**

## 9. Design Goal 3: Being Open

### 9.1 Definition

> An **open distributed system** is essentially a system that offers **components** that can easily be **used by, or integrated into** other systems. At the same time, an open distributed system will often itself consist of components that **originate from elsewhere.**

### 9.2 Interoperability, Portability, and Extensibility

#### Interface Definition Languages (IDLs)
- Being open means components should adhere to **standard rules** describing the **syntax and semantics** of what those components offer.
- A general approach: define services through interfaces using an **Interface Definition Language (IDL)** — which nearly always captures only the **syntax** of services (function names, parameter types, return values, possible exceptions).
- **The hard part:** specifying precisely **what** those services **do** — the **semantics.** In practice, these specifications are given **informally**, via natural language.

#### Complete and Neutral Specifications
- If **properly specified**, an interface definition allows arbitrary processes needing a certain interface to **talk to another process providing that interface**, and allows **two independent parties to build completely different implementations** that operate exactly the same way.
- **Complete:** everything necessary to make an implementation has been specified. *(Many interface definitions are NOT complete, requiring developers to add implementation-specific details.)*
- **Neutral:** specifications don't prescribe what an implementation should look like.

#### Interoperability vs. Portability

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Interoperability** | The extent to which **two implementations** of systems/components from **different manufacturers** can co-exist and work together, relying merely on each other's services as specified by a **common standard.** |
| **Portability** | The extent to which an application developed for distributed system A can be executed, **without modification**, on a **different** distributed system B that implements the **same interfaces** as A. |

#### Extensibility
- An open distributed system should be **easy to configure** out of different components (possibly from different developers).
- It should be **easy to add new components, or replace existing ones**, without affecting components that stay in place — i.e., it should be **extensible.**

### 9.3 The Open Source Route

- Practice shows many distributed systems are **not as open as we'd like**, and a lot of effort is needed to put various bits and pieces together.
- **One way out:** simply reveal all the "gory details" of a component and provide developers with the **actual source code** — this is the **open source** approach, increasingly popular, where large groups of people **contribute to improving and debugging systems.** *(Admittedly as open as a system can get — but "if it is the best way is questionable."* )

### 9.4 Separating Policy from Mechanism

> To achieve flexibility in open distributed systems, it's crucial that the system be organized as a **collection of relatively small and easily replaceable or adaptable components.**

- We should provide definitions not only of the **highest-level interfaces** (seen by users/applications), but also definitions for interfaces to **internal parts** of the system, and describe how those parts interact.
- **Older/monolithic systems** are constructed with components only **logically separated**, but implemented as **one huge program** — this makes it **hard to replace or adapt** a component without affecting the entire system. **Monolithic systems thus tend to be closed instead of open.**

#### Worked Example — Web Browser Caching

- Multiple parameters must be considered:
    1. **Storage:** where is data cached (in-memory, on disk, and where on disk)?
    2. **Exemption:** when the cache fills up, which data is removed?
    3. **Sharing:** private cache per browser, or shared among browsers of different users?
    4. **Refreshing:** when does a browser check if cached data is still up-to-date? *(Caches are most effective when the browser can return pages without contacting the original site — but this risks returning stale data. Refresh rates depend heavily on what's cached: train timetables barely change, but highway-traffic pages or stock prices change constantly.)*

- **What's needed: separation between policy and mechanism.** Ideally, a browser provides facilities for **only storing** documents, while letting users decide **which** documents are stored and **for how long.**
- **In practice:** implemented by offering a **rich set of parameters** users can (dynamically) set — a browser may even offer facilities for **plugging in policies** implemented as separate components.

#### The Trade-off of Strict Separation

> The **stricter** the separation, the **more** we need to make sure we offer the appropriate collection of mechanisms.

- This typically means a **rich set of features**, leading to **many configuration parameters.** Example: **Firefox comes with a few hundred configuration parameters.** Just imagine how the configuration space explodes for large distributed systems with many components.
- **In other words: strict separation of policies and mechanisms may lead to highly complex configuration problems.**

#### Mitigations

1. **Provide reasonable defaults** — what often happens in practice.
2. **Self-configuring systems** — the system observes its own usage and dynamically changes parameter settings; receiving increasing research/practitioner interest.

> **Hardcoding policies into a distributed system may reduce complexity considerably, but at the price of less flexibility.** Finding the right balance is one of the reasons why designing a distributed system is **"sometimes more an art than a science."**

## 10. Design Goal 4: Being Scalable

### 10.1 Why Scalability Matters More Than Ever

> For many of us, worldwide connectivity through the Internet is as common as being able to send a postcard to anyone anywhere in the world. [...] With this in mind, **scalability has become one of the most important design goals** for developers of distributed systems.

### 10.2 The Three Scalability Dimensions (per Neuman)

| Dimension | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Size scalability** | We can easily **add more users and resources** to the system without any **noticeable loss of performance.** |
| **Geographical scalability** | Users and resources **may lie far apart**, but the fact that communication delays may be significant is **hardly noticed.** |
| **Administrative scalability** | The system can **still be easily managed** even if it **spans many independent administrative organizations.** |

### 10.3 Size Scalability — Root Causes of Bottlenecks

When a service is implemented on a single machine, there are essentially **three root causes** for becoming a bottleneck:

1. **The computational capacity**, limited by the CPUs.
2. **The storage capacity**, including the transfer rate between CPUs and disks.
3. **The network** between the user and the centralized service.

**Example — computational bottleneck:** a service computing optimal routes with real-time traffic information may be a **compute-bound service**, requiring several (sometimes tens of) seconds to complete a request — even a modern high-end single machine will eventually run into problems as request volume grows.

**Example — I/O bottleneck:** a poorly designed centralized search engine, needing to match a query against an **entire dataset**. Even with advanced indexing, we may face processing a huge amount of data exceeding **main-memory capacity** — much of the processing time is determined by **relatively slow disk accesses** and data transfer between disk and memory. *"Simply adding more or higher-speed disks will prove not to be a sustainable solution."*

**Example — network bottleneck:** a video-on-demand service streaming high-quality video (easily 8–10 Mbps per stream) to multiple users — a service setting up point-to-point connections may soon hit the limits of its own **outgoing transmission line capacity.**

### 10.4 A Formal Queuing-Theory Analysis of Size Scalability

> Size scalability problems for centralized services can be formally analyzed using **queuing theory**, making a few simplifying assumptions.

**Model:** requests are submitted to the service, where they are **queued** until the process can handle an extra request, fetches it, does its work, and produces a response — modeled as a simple **queuing system.**

**Assumptions:**
- **Infinite queue capacity** — arrival rate isn't influenced by what's currently in the queue.
- **Arrival rate** = λ requests/second; **processing capacity** = μ requests/second.

**Derivation:**

The fraction of time p_k that there are k requests in the system:
$$
p_k = \left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{\mu}\right)\left(\frac{\lambda}{\mu}\right)^k
$$

Defining **utilization U** as the fraction of time the service is busy:
$$
U = \sum_{k>0} p_k = 1 - p_0 = \frac{\lambda}{\mu} \implies p_k = (1-U)U^k
$$

The **average number of requests N** in the system:
$$
N = \sum_{k \geq 0} k \cdot p_k = (1-U)\sum_{k \geq 0} k \cdot U^k = \frac{(1-U)U}{(1-U)^2} = \frac{U}{1-U}
$$

The **average throughput X**: since the service is "busy" (processing at μ requests/second) a fraction U of the time, and idle the rest:
$$
X = U \cdot \mu + (1-U) \cdot 0 = \frac{\lambda}{\mu} \cdot \mu = \lambda
$$

Using **Little's Law**, the **response time R**:
$$
R = \frac{N}{X} = \frac{S}{1-U} \implies \frac{R}{S} = \frac{1}{1-U}
$$

where **S = 1/μ**, the actual service time.

**Interpretation:**
- If **U is very small**, the response-to-service time ratio is **close to 1** — a request is virtually instantly processed, at maximum speed possible.
- As **U approaches 1**, the response-to-service time ratio **quickly increases to very high values** — the system is coming close to a **grinding halt.** *This is where scalability problems emerge.*
- **From this simple model, the only solution is bringing down the service time S.**

### 10.5 Geographical Scalability — The Problems

#### Synchronous Communication's Limitation
- Many distributed systems designed for LANs are based on **synchronous communication**: a client **blocks** until a reply is sent back from the server.
- This works fine in LANs, where communication is often at worst a **few hundred μs.** But in wide-area systems, interprocess communication may be **hundreds of milliseconds — three orders of magnitude slower.**
- Building applications using synchronous communication in wide-area systems requires **great care (and not just a little patience)**, notably with rich client-server interaction patterns.

#### Reliability and Bandwidth
- Communication in WANs is **inherently much less reliable** than in LANs, and we also need to deal with **limited bandwidth.**
- **Example — streaming video:** trivial in a home network (even wireless); simply placing the media server far away and using standard TCP will **surely fail** — bandwidth limitations instantly surface, and maintaining reliability causes headaches.

#### Limited Multipoint Communication
- Wide-area systems generally have **only very limited facilities for multipoint communication.** In contrast, LANs often support **efficient broadcasting mechanisms**, useful for **discovering components and services.**
- In WANs, **separate services** (naming and directory services) must be developed, which themselves need to be scalable — and in many cases, **no obvious solutions exist.**

### 10.6 Administrative Scalability — The Hardest Dimension

> A difficult, and in many cases **open**, question is how to scale a distributed system across multiple, **independent administrative domains.**

- **Major problem:** **conflicting policies** with respect to resource usage (and payment), management, and security.

#### Worked Example — Computational Grids
- Scientists have long sought to **share expensive equipment** in what's known as a **computational grid** — a global distributed system constructed as a **federation of local distributed systems**, letting a program at organization A **directly access resources** at organization B.

#### Trust Doesn't Cross Domain Boundaries
- Components within a **single domain** are often trusted by users operating within that same domain (system administration may have tested/certified applications, taken measures against tampering) — *"users trust their system administrators."* **This trust does NOT expand naturally across domain boundaries.**
- **Two types of security measures needed when expanding to another domain:**
    1. The distributed system must **protect itself against malicious attacks from the new domain** (e.g., new-domain users may get only read access to files in the original domain).
    2. The **new domain must protect itself against malicious attacks from the distributed system** — e.g., downloading **applets** in web browsers; the new domain doesn't know what to expect from foreign code and may **severely limit access rights.** *The problem is HOW to enforce those limitations.*

#### Counter-Example — Peer-to-Peer Networks
- Modern file-sharing P2P networks **apparently don't suffer from administrative scalability problems**: end users simply install a program and, within minutes, can start downloading files.
- Other examples: P2P telephony (**Skype**), peer-assisted audio streaming (early **Spotify**).
- **What they have in common:** **end users**, not administrative entities, **collaborate to keep the system up and running.** At best, ISPs can **police network traffic**, but such efforts have so far **not been very effective.**

### 10.7 Scaling Techniques

> In most cases, scalability problems appear as **performance problems** caused by limited capacity of servers and network.

#### Scaling UP vs. Scaling OUT
- **Scaling up:** simply improving capacity (increasing memory, upgrading CPUs, replacing network modules) — often a solution.
- **Scaling out:** expanding the distributed system by **deploying more machines.** There are basically **three techniques**: hiding communication latencies, distribution of work, and replication.

#### Technique 1 — Hiding Communication Latencies

> Applicable in the case of **geographical scalability.** The basic idea: **try to avoid waiting for responses** to remote-service requests as much as possible.

- **Approach A — asynchronous communication:** when a service is requested at a remote machine, do **other useful work** at the requester's side instead of waiting. When a reply comes in, the application is **interrupted** and a **special handler** completes the previously issued request. Often used in **batch-processing systems and parallel applications.**
- **Approach B — a new thread of control:** blocks waiting for the reply, but **other threads** in the process can continue.
- **Limitation:** many applications (e.g., interactive ones) **cannot** effectively use asynchronous communication — a user who sends a request generally has nothing better to do than **wait for the answer.**
- **Approach C — reduce overall communication:** move part of the computation normally done at the server to the **client process** requesting the service.

**Worked Example — Filling in a Web Form**

```
(a) Server-side checking (slower, more chatty):
Client                                    Server
[FIRSTNAME MAARTEN]  --field-by-field-->  [Check each field,
[LASTNAME VAN STEEN]  --with ack each--    acknowledge, process form]
[E-MAIL]

(b) Client-side checking (faster, less chatty):
Client                                    Server
[Code for checking form SHIPPED to client]
[FIRSTNAME MAARTEN]
[LASTNAME VAN STEEN]  --entire completed-->  [Process form]
[E-MAIL]                  form sent once
```

- **(a)** Sends a **separate message for each field**, waiting for an acknowledgement (e.g., the server checks for syntactic errors before accepting an entry).
- **(b)** **Ships the code** for filling in the form (and checking entries) to the client, which returns a **completed form** — a **much better solution.**

#### Technique 2 — Partitioning and Distribution

> Taking a component, **splitting it into smaller parts**, and subsequently **spreading those parts** across the system.

**Worked Example — The Internet DNS**

- The **DNS namespace** is hierarchically organized into a **tree of domains**, divided into **nonoverlapping zones.** Names in each zone are handled by a **single name server.**
- **Resolving `flits.cs.vu.nl`:** first passed to the server of zone **Z1**, which returns the address of the server for zone **Z2**, to which the rest of the name (`flits.cs.vu`) is handed. The server for **Z2** returns the address of the server for zone **Z3**, which handles the last part and returns the address of the associated host.

```
DNS Zone Hierarchy (example):
                    Z1 (root-ish)
                   /    |    \
              com   edu  ...  nl
                          /      \
                        vu    (other .nl domains)   ← Z2
                       /
                     cs                              ← Z3
                    / | \
              flits ... (other cs.vu.nl hosts)
```

- **Illustrates how the naming service, as provided by DNS, is distributed across several machines**, avoiding a single server having to deal with all name-resolution requests.

**Worked Example — The World Wide Web**

- To most users, the Web appears as an **enormous document-based information system** where each document has a unique URL — conceptually as if there were **only a single server.**
- However, the Web is **physically partitioned and distributed** across a **few hundred million servers**, each handling a number of web documents. The name of the server handling a document is **encoded into that document's URL.**
- *"It is only because of this distribution of documents that the Web has been capable of scaling to its current size."*

#### Technique 3 — Replication

> Because scalability problems often appear as **performance degradation**, it's generally a good idea to **replicate** components across a distributed system.

- **Benefits:** increases **availability**; helps **balance load** between components (better performance); in geographically dispersed systems, having a copy **nearby** can hide much of the communication latency problem.

**Caching — A Special Form of Replication**
- Similar to replication (makes a copy of a resource, generally near the **client** accessing it), but **caching is a decision made by the CLIENT of a resource**, not the **owner** of a resource (which is who decides on replication).

**The Serious Drawback — Consistency**
> Because we now have **multiple copies** of a resource, modifying one copy makes it **different from the others.** Consequently, **caching and replication lead to consistency problems.**

- **How much inconsistency can be tolerated depends heavily on resource usage.** Many web users find it acceptable that a browser returns a **cached document** whose validity hasn't been checked for a few minutes. But **strong consistency guarantees** are needed in cases like **electronic stock exchanges and auctions.**
- **The problem with strong consistency:** an update must be **immediately propagated to all other copies.** If two updates happen **concurrently**, updates must often also be processed in the **same order everywhere** — an additional **global ordering problem.**
- **The CAP problem/theorem:** *combining consistency, availability, and being tolerant to network partitions is NOT possible.*
- Replication therefore often requires **global synchronization mechanisms** — extremely hard or impossible to implement in a scalable way, since **network latencies have a natural lower bound.** *Consequently, scaling by replication may introduce OTHER, inherently non-scalable solutions.*

### 10.8 Discussion — Ranking the Difficulty of the Three Scalability Dimensions

| Dimension | Relative Difficulty | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Size scalability** | **Least problematic**, technically | In many cases, increasing a machine's capacity "will save the day," although perhaps at a high monetary cost. |
| **Geographical scalability** | **Much tougher** | Network latencies are naturally bound from below — we're forced to copy data closer to clients, leading to consistency-maintenance problems. Practice shows combining distribution, replication, and caching with different consistency forms generally leads to acceptable solutions. |
| **Administrative scalability** | **Most difficult** | We must deal with **nontechnical issues** — organizational politics and human collaboration. Peer-to-peer technology has successfully demonstrated what's achievable when **end users are put in control** — though P2P is "obviously not the universal solution to all administrative scalability problems." |

## 11. Pitfalls — Peter Deutsch's False Assumptions

### 11.1 The Core Warning

> It should be clear by now that developing a distributed system is a **formidable task.** [...] Distributed systems differ from traditional software because components are **dispersed across a network.** **Not taking this dispersion into account during design time is what makes so many systems needlessly complex** and results in flaws that need to be patched later on.

### 11.2 The Eight False Assumptions (Peter Deutsch, Sun Microsystems)

> These are the **false assumptions that everyone makes** when developing a distributed application **for the first time**:

1. **The network is reliable**
2. **The network is secure**
3. **The network is homogeneous**
4. **The topology does not change**
5. **Latency is zero**
6. **Bandwidth is infinite**
7. **Transport cost is zero**
8. **There is one administrator**

### 11.3 What These Relate To

> Note how these assumptions relate to properties **unique to distributed systems**: reliability, security, heterogeneity, and topology of the network; latency and bandwidth; transport costs; and finally administrative domains. **When developing nondistributed applications, most of these issues will most likely not show up.**

```
Deutsch's Eight Fallacies, mapped to underlying distributed-systems property:

1. Network is reliable        ──►  Reliability
2. Network is secure          ──►  Security
3. Network is homogeneous     ──►  Heterogeneity
4. Topology does not change   ──►  Network topology
5. Latency is zero            ──►  Latency
6. Bandwidth is infinite      ──►  Bandwidth
7. Transport cost is zero     ──►  Transport costs
8. There is one administrator ──►  Administrative domains
```

## 13. High-Performance Distributed Computing

### 13.1 Two Subgroups

| Subgroup | Description |
|---|---|
| **Cluster computing** | Underlying hardware = a collection of **similar** workstations/PCs, closely connected by a **high-speed LAN.** Each node runs the **same operating system.** |
| **Grid computing** | Distributed systems constructed as a **federation of computer systems**, where each system may fall under a **different administrative domain**, and may be very different in hardware, software, and network technology. |

- **From grid computing, a next logical step:** simply **outsource the entire infrastructure** needed for compute-intensive applications — this is what **cloud computing** is all about.
- **Unlike grid computing** (strongly associated with high-performance computing), **cloud computing is much more than just providing lots of resources.**

### 13.2 Multiprocessors vs. Multicomputers — The Origin

- **High-performance computing** more or less started with **multiprocessor machines** — multiple CPUs organized so they **all have access to the same physical memory.**
- In contrast, in a **multicomputer** system, several computers are connected through a network and there is **no sharing of main memory.**

```
Shared Memory (Multiprocessor):        Private Memory (Multicomputer):
   M  M  M  M                              P─M  P─M  P─M  P─M
    \  \ | /                                 \    |    |   /
    [Interconnect]                          [Interconnect]
     |  |  |  |
     P  P  P  P
```

- The **shared-memory model** proved highly convenient for improving performance and was **relatively easy to program.**
- **Essence of shared-memory parallel programs:** multiple threads of control execute at the same time, all with access to **shared data**, controlled through **well-understood synchronization mechanisms** (e.g., semaphores).
- **Unfortunately, the model does not easily scale:** so far, machines have been developed where only **a few tens of CPUs** have efficient access to shared memory. To a certain extent, we see the **same limitations for multicore processors** (some of which are multiprocessors, some not).

### 13.3 Distributed Shared Memory (DSM) — A Cautionary Tale

- To overcome shared-memory limitations, high-performance computing **moved to distributed-memory systems.** This meant many programs had to use **message passing** instead of modifying shared data.
- **Unfortunately**, message-passing models proved **much more difficult and error-prone** compared to shared-memory programming models. This drove significant research into **Distributed Shared-Memory (DSM) multicomputers.**
- **How DSM works:** allows a processor to address a memory location at another computer **as if it were local memory** — e.g., mapping all main-memory pages of various processors into a **single virtual address space.** When processor A addresses a page located at processor B, a **page fault** occurs at A, letting A's OS fetch the content of the referenced page from B, the same way it would normally fetch it locally from disk. Meanwhile, B is informed the page is currently **not accessible.**
- **The eventual outcome:** *"This elegant idea of mimicking shared-memory systems using multicomputers eventually had to be abandoned"* — performance could never meet programmer expectations, who'd rather resort to **far more intricate, yet better (predictably) performing message-passing** models.
- **An important side-effect:** exploring these hardware-software boundaries gave a **thorough understanding of consistency models.**

### 13.4 Cluster Computing

- Became popular as the **price/performance ratio** of PCs and workstations improved — at a certain point, it became financially/technically attractive to **build a supercomputer using off-the-shelf technology**, simply hooking up simple computers in a high-speed network.
- **Virtually always used for parallel programming**, where a **single (compute-intensive) program** is run in parallel on multiple machines.

#### Worked Example — Linux-Based Beowulf Clusters

```
       Master Node                Compute Node    Compute Node    Compute Node
   [Management application] ─┬──► [Component of  ][Component of  ][Component of
   (runs the middleware)     │     parallel app]   parallel app]   parallel app]
                              │
                              │    [Parallel libs] [Parallel libs] [Parallel libs]
              High-speed network ── [Local OS]      [Local OS]      [Local OS]
```

- Each cluster consists of **compute nodes**, controlled/accessed via a **single master node.**
- **The master:** handles allocation of nodes to a particular parallel program, maintains a **batch queue** of submitted jobs, provides an interface for users — the master runs the **middleware** needed for execution/management.
- **Compute nodes:** equipped with a standard OS extended with middleware functions for **communication, storage, fault tolerance**, etc. — otherwise **highly identical.**

#### An Even More Symmetric Approach — MOSIX

- **MOSIX** attempts to provide a **single-system image** of a cluster — appearing to a process to be a **single computer**, offering the **ultimate distribution transparency.**
- *(As mentioned, providing such an image under ALL circumstances is impossible.)*
- **Mechanism:** processes can **dynamically and preemptively migrate** between nodes making up the cluster. A user starts an application on the **home node**, after which it can **transparently move** to other nodes (e.g., for efficient resource use).

#### The Shift Toward Hybrid Architectures

- Several modern cluster computers have moved from these **symmetric architectures** to **hybrid solutions**, where middleware is **functionally partitioned across different nodes.**
- **Advantage:** compute nodes with **dedicated, lightweight OSes** provide optimal performance for compute-intensive applications; storage functionality can be optimally handled by **specially configured** file/directory-server nodes; the same holds for other dedicated middleware services (job management, database services, general Internet access).

### 13.5 Grid Computing

#### The Defining Diversity
- A characteristic of **traditional cluster computing is its homogeneity.** Grid computing shows **even more diversity**: **no assumptions** are made concerning similarity of hardware, OSes, networks, administrative domains, or security policies.

#### Virtual Organizations
- **Key issue:** resources from **different organizations** are brought together to allow **collaboration of a group of people** from different institutions — forming a **federation of systems.**
- Such collaboration is realized in the form of a **virtual organization.** Processes belonging to the same virtual organization have **access rights** to the resources provided to that organization.
- **Typical resources:** compute servers (including supercomputers, possibly implemented as clusters), storage facilities, databases, and **special networked devices** (telescopes, sensors, etc.).

#### The Foster et al. Grid Reference Architecture (Four Layers)

```
        Application Layer
              │
        Collective Layer
              │
        Connectivity Layer   Resource Layer
              │
          Fabric Layer
```

| Layer | Role |
|---|---|
| **Fabric** (lowest) | Provides **interfaces to local resources** at a specific site, tailored to allow **sharing** within a virtual organization. Typically: functions for **querying state/capabilities** of a resource, plus functions for **resource management** (e.g., locking resources). |
| **Connectivity** | Consists of **communication protocols** supporting grid transactions spanning multiple resources (e.g., transferring data, remote resource access) plus **security protocols** to **authenticate users and resources.** Note: often **PROGRAMS acting on behalf of users** are authenticated, not human users directly — **delegating rights** from user to program is an important supported function. |
| **Resource** | Responsible for **managing a single resource**, using functions from Connectivity and calling directly the interfaces from Fabric. Offers functions for obtaining **configuration information**, or performing specific operations (creating a process, reading data). Responsible for **access control**, relying on authentication from the Connectivity layer. |
| **Collective** | Deals with handling access to **multiple resources**: services for **resource discovery, allocation and scheduling of tasks across multiple resources, data replication**, etc. Unlike the Connectivity/Resource layers (relatively small, standard protocol collections), the Collective layer **may consist of many different protocols**, reflecting the broad spectrum of services it may offer a virtual organization. |
| **Application** (topmost) | Consists of the **applications** operating within a virtual organization, making use of the grid computing environment. |

- **Typically**, the Collective, Connectivity, and Resource layers form the heart of what could be called a **grid middleware layer.**
- **Trend:** a gradual shift toward a **service-oriented architecture**, in which sites offer access to the various layers through **Web services** — leading to the **Open Grid Services Architecture (OGSA)**, based on Foster et al.'s original ideas but, having gone through a **standardization process**, is "complex, to say the least." OGSA implementations generally **follow Web service standards.**

### 13.6 Cloud Computing

#### From Utility Computing to Cloud Computing
- While researchers explored computational grids, organizations running datacenters faced the problem of **opening up their resources to customers** — leading to **utility computing**, where a customer could **upload tasks** to a datacenter and be **charged on a per-resource basis.** Utility computing formed the basis for **cloud computing.**

#### Definition
> Cloud computing is characterized by an **easily usable and accessible pool of virtualized resources.** Which and how resources are used can be **configured dynamically**, providing the basis for **scalability** — if more work needs to be done, a customer can simply **acquire more resources.**

- **Link to utility computing:** generally based on a **pay-per-use model**, with guarantees offered via **customized service level agreements (SLAs).**

#### The Four Cloud Layers

```
Application  ──►  Google Docs, YouTube, Flickr, Web services, multimedia, business apps
Platform     ──►  MS Azure, Google App Engine — Software framework (Java/Python/.Net), storage (databases)
Infrastructure ─►  Amazon EC2, Amazon S3 — Computation (VM), Storage (block)
Hardware     ──►  Datacenters — CPU, memory, disk, bandwidth
```

| Layer | Description |
|---|---|
| **Hardware** (lowest) | Manages the necessary hardware: processors, routers, power/cooling systems. Implemented at datacenters, containing resources customers **normally never get to see directly.** |
| **Infrastructure** | The backbone for most cloud-computing platforms; deploys **virtualization techniques** to provide customers virtual storage and computing resources — *"nothing is what it seems: cloud computing evolves around allocating and managing virtual storage devices and virtual servers."* |
| **Platform** | Provides to a cloud-computing customer what an **operating system provides to application developers**: the means to easily develop/deploy applications running in a cloud. A developer is offered a **vendor-specific API** — comparable to the Unix `exec` family of system calls. Also provides **higher-level abstractions for storage** — e.g., **Amazon S3**, offered via an API letting locally-created files be organized/stored in **buckets** (comparable to a directory) — storing a file in a bucket **automatically uploads it** to the Amazon cloud. |
| **Application** (topmost) | Actual applications run here, offered to users for further customization — e.g., office suites (text processors, spreadsheets, presentations). These run **in the vendor's cloud.** |

#### Three Service Types

- **Infrastructure-as-a-Service (IaaS)** — covering the hardware and infrastructure layer.
- **Platform-as-a-Service (PaaS)** — covering the platform layer.
- **Software-as-a-Service (SaaS)** — covering the applications themselves.

#### Obstacles

> As of now, making use of clouds is relatively easy. [...] However, there are still a number of serious obstacles including **provider lock-in, security and privacy issues, and dependency on the availability of services**, to mention a few.

- Because details of how specific cloud computations are actually carried out are **generally hidden**, and even perhaps **unknown or unpredictable**, meeting performance demands may be **impossible to arrange in advance.**
- **Li et al. have shown** that different providers may easily show **very different performance profiles.**
- *"Cloud computing is no longer a hype, and certainly a serious alternative to maintaining huge local infrastructures, yet there is still a lot of room for improvement."*

## 14. Distributed Information Systems

### 14.1 The Motivating Problem

> Another important class of distributed systems is found in organizations confronted with a **wealth of networked applications**, but for which **interoperability turned out to be a painful experience.** Many existing middleware solutions are the result of working with an infrastructure in which it was **easier to integrate applications** into an enterprise-wide information system.

### 14.2 Distributed Transaction Processing

#### The Basic Model
- A networked application often consists of a **server** running the application (often including a database), made available to **remote programs** (**clients**). Clients send **requests**; a **response** is sent back.
- Integration at the lowest level allows clients to wrap multiple requests (possibly for different servers) into a **single larger request**, executed as a **distributed transaction** — the key idea: **all, or none** of the requests are executed.

#### Transaction Primitives (Table 2)

| Primitive | Description |
|---|---|
| **BEGIN_TRANSACTION** | Mark the start of a transaction. |
| **END_TRANSACTION** | Terminate the transaction and try to **commit.** |
| **ABORT_TRANSACTION** | **Kill** the transaction and restore the old values. |
| **READ** | Read data from a file, a table, or otherwise. |
| **WRITE** | Write data to a file, a table, or otherwise. |

- The exact list of primitives depends on what kinds of objects are used — e.g., a mail system might have primitives to **send, receive, and forward mail.** **Remote Procedure Calls (RPCs)** are often also encapsulated in a transaction, leading to a **transactional RPC.**

#### The ACID Properties

> The **all-or-nothing property** is one of **four characteristic properties** transactions have:

| Property | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Atomic** | To the outside world, the transaction happens **indivisibly.** |
| **Consistent** | The transaction does **not violate system invariants.** |
| **Isolated** | **Concurrent** transactions do not interfere with each other. |
| **Durable** | Once a transaction **commits**, the changes are **permanent.** |

#### Nested Transactions

```
                Nested Transaction (top-level)
                    /                \
           Subtransaction         Subtransaction
          (Airline database)      (Hotel database)
                          Two different (independent) databases
```

- In distributed systems, transactions are often constructed as **subtransactions**, jointly forming a **nested transaction.**
- The top-level transaction may **fork off children** running in parallel with one another, on **different machines**, to gain performance or simplify programming. Each child may itself execute further subtransactions, or fork off its own children.

#### The Subtle Problem — Committed Subtransactions Under an Aborted Parent

- Imagine a transaction starts several subtransactions in parallel, and **one commits**, making its results **visible to the parent.** After further computation, the **parent aborts**, restoring the entire system to the state it had before the top-level transaction started. **The results of the subtransaction that committed must nevertheless be undone.**
- **Thus, "permanence" applies only to TOP-LEVEL transactions.**
- **The private-universe semantics:** when any transaction/subtransaction starts, it's conceptually given a **private copy of all data** in the entire system to manipulate as it wishes. If it **aborts**, its private universe **just vanishes**, as if it never existed. If it **commits**, its private universe **replaces the parent's universe.** Thus if a subtransaction commits and then a NEW subtransaction starts, the second one **sees the results produced by the first.** Likewise, if an **enclosing (higher-level) transaction aborts, all underlying subtransactions must abort as well.** If several transactions start concurrently, the result is **as if they ran sequentially in some unspecified order.**

- **Nested transactions are important in distributed systems**, providing a **natural way of distributing a transaction across multiple machines**, following a **logical division of work.**
- **Worked example:** a transaction planning a trip requiring **three different flights** can be logically split into **three subtransactions**, each managed **separately and independently** of the other two.

#### The TP Monitor

- In early enterprise middleware, the component handling distributed (or nested) transactions formed the **core for integrating applications** at the server/database level — the **transaction processing monitor**, or **TP monitor.**
- Its main task: allow an application to access **multiple server/databases** by offering it a **transactional programming model**, coordinating **commitment of subtransactions** following a standard protocol known as **distributed commit.**

```
                     Server
                       │
Client application  Requests   Reply
       │              │          ▲
       └──► TP monitor ──────────┘
              │           Requests   Reply
              ├────────► Server ◄────┘
              │
              └────────► Server (etc.)
```

- **Important observation:** applications wanting to coordinate several subtransactions into a single transaction did **NOT** have to implement this coordination themselves — using a TP monitor, this coordination is **done for them.** *"This is exactly where middleware comes into play: it implements services that are useful for many applications, avoiding that such services have to be reimplemented over and over again by application developers."*

### 14.3 Enterprise Application Integration (EAI)

#### The Need for Direct Communication
- As applications became decoupled from the databases they were built on, it became clear that integration should also let **application components communicate directly with each other** — not merely via the request/reply behavior of transaction processing systems.

#### Communication Middleware Types

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **RPC (Remote Procedure Call)** | An application component can effectively send a request to another by doing a **local procedure call**, resulting in the request being **packaged as a message** and sent to the callee. The result is sent back and returned as the result of the procedure call. |
| **RMI (Remote Method Invocation)** | Essentially the same as RPC, except it operates on **objects** instead of functions — emerged as **object technology** grew popular. |
| **MOM (Message-Oriented Middleware)** | Addresses the disadvantage of RPC/RMI, which require **both caller and callee up and running** at the time of communication (a "tight coupling" often experienced as a serious drawback). In MOM, applications send messages to **logical contact points** (often described by a **subject**), and can indicate interest for specific message types — the communication middleware ensures delivery. These **publish/subscribe systems** form an important and expanding class of distributed systems. |

### 14.4 Four Ways to Integrate Applications

| Approach | How It Works | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| **File transfer** | An application produces a file with shared data, subsequently read by others. **Technically very simple.** | Many things need agreeing on: **file format/layout** (text, binary, structure — XML has become popular since files are self-describing); **file management** (storage location, naming, deletion responsibility); **update propagation** (may need separate programs to notify applications of file updates). |
| **Shared database** | All applications access the same data, often via a **high-level language like SQL.** Easy to notify applications of changes via **triggers.** | Two major drawbacks: (1) still need a **common data schema**, potentially non-trivial if the full set of applications isn't known in advance; (2) with many reads/updates, a shared database can easily become a **performance bottleneck.** |
| **Remote procedure call (RPC)** | For cases where small changes should trigger **many applications** to take actions — captured by **executing a procedure.** Using standard encapsulation techniques (traditional procedure calls / object invocations), an application offers a procedure to other applications as an RPC. Lets application A make use of information available only to B, **without giving A direct access** to that information. | Requires caller and callee to be **up and running simultaneously.** |
| **Messaging** | Addresses RPC's simultaneity requirement — a **messaging system** carries requests from A to perform an action at B, ensuring **eventual delivery**, and (if needed) that a response is eventually returned. | Not a panacea: introduces problems concerning **data formatting/layout**, requires an application to know **where to send** a message, and needs scenarios for dealing with **lost messages.** |

> *"What these four approaches tell us, is that application integration will generally not be simple."*

## 15. Pervasive Systems

### 15.1 The Shift From Stable Systems to Pervasive Ones

- Distributed systems discussed so far are largely characterized by **stability**: nodes are fixed and have a permanent, high-quality network connection. This stability is realized through **distribution transparency techniques.**
- **However**, matters changed with **mobile and embedded computing devices**, leading to **pervasive systems** — intended to **naturally blend into our environment.**
- **What makes them unique:** the separation between **users and system components** is **much more blurred.** There's often **no single dedicated interface** (screen/keyboard) — instead, a pervasive system is often equipped with **many sensors** picking up various aspects of user behavior, and **many actuators** providing feedback (or purposefully steering behavior).

### 15.2 Common Device Characteristics

- **Small, battery-powered, mobile**, with often **only a wireless connection** — though not all characteristics apply to all devices (e.g., **smartphones** aren't necessarily restricted).
- Because we often need to deal with the intricacies of **wireless and mobile communication**, special solutions are needed to make a pervasive system as **transparent or unobtrusive** as possible.

### 15.3 Three Types (With Considerable Overlap)

1. **Ubiquitous computing systems**
2. **Mobile computing systems**
3. **Sensor networks**

### 15.4 Ubiquitous Computing Systems

> A ubiquitous computing system takes pervasiveness a step further: the system is **pervasive AND continuously present** — a user will be **continuously interacting** with the system, often not even aware interaction is taking place.

#### Poslad's Five Core Requirements

| # | Requirement | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Distribution** | Devices are networked, distributed, and **transparently accessible.** |
| 2 | **Interaction** | Interaction between users and devices is **highly unobtrusive.** |
| 3 | **Context awareness** | The system is aware of a **user's context** to optimize interaction. |
| 4 | **Autonomy** | Devices operate **autonomously**, without human intervention — **highly self-managed.** |
| 5 | **Intelligence** | The system as a whole can handle a **wide range of dynamic actions and interactions.** |

#### Ad. 1 — Distribution
- A ubiquitous computing system is an example of a distributed system: devices/computers forming its nodes are networked, working together to form the **illusion of a single coherent system.**
- Distribution comes naturally: devices close to users (sensors, actuators), connected to computers hidden from view, perhaps operating remotely in a **cloud.**

#### Ad. 2 — Interaction (Implicit Human-Computer Interaction)
- End users play a **prominent role** in ubiquitous system design — much interaction will be **implicit**: an **implicit action** is one *"that is not primarily aimed to interact with a computerized system but which such a system understands as input."*
- **Worked example — a car's personalized seat/steering wheel/mirror settings:** if Bob takes a seat, the system recognizes him and makes appropriate adjustments; the same happens for Alice; an unknown user is steered toward making their own adjustments (remembered for later). Sensors act as **input devices** identifying a situation (a specific person wanting to drive), whose analysis leads to **actions** (adjustments) — which may lead to natural reactions (Bob slightly changes the seat settings), which the system must also take into account.

#### Ad. 3 — Context Awareness
- **Dey and Abowd's definition:** context is *"any information that can be used to characterize the situation of entities (i.e., whether a person, place or object) that are considered relevant to the interaction between a user and an application, including the user and the application themselves."*
- **In practice**, context is often characterized by **location, identity, time, and activity** — the **where, who, when, and what.**
- **What's important from a distributed-systems perspective:** raw sensor data must be **lifted to a level of abstraction** usable by applications. **Example:** detecting a person's location via GPS coordinates, then mapping that to an actual location (a street corner, a specific shop). **The question:** where does this processing happen — centrally, or on the user's smartphone? *There are trade-offs to consider.*
- **Shared dataspaces** (where processes are decoupled in time/space) are attractive for combining flexibility and distribution, but **suffer from scalability problems.**

#### Ad. 4 — Autonomy
- In a ubiquitous computing environment, there's **simply no room for a systems administrator** to keep everything up and running — the system must **act autonomously**, automatically reacting to changes.
- **Examples of the required techniques:**
    - **Address allocation:** e.g., via **DHCP** (requires a server) or **Zeroconf.**
    - **Adding devices:** via protocols like **Universal Plug and Play (UPnP)**, letting devices discover each other and set up communication channels.
    - **Automatic updates:** devices regularly check via the Internet if software should be updated, downloading new versions and ideally continuing where they left off.

#### Ad. 5 — Intelligence
- Ubiquitous computing systems often use methods from **artificial intelligence**, handling **incomplete input**, reacting quickly to a **changing environment**, and handling **unexpected events.**
- **A tension:** distributed solutions for many AI problems are **yet to be found**, creating tension between the requirement of networked/distributed devices and **advanced distributed information processing.**

### 15.5 Mobile Computing Systems

#### What Sets Mobile Computing Apart From Pervasive Systems Generally

**Feature 1 — Device Diversity**
- Devices vary widely: **smartphones, tablets**, but also **remote controls, pagers, active badges, car equipment, GPS-enabled devices**, etc. — a characteristic feature: they use **wireless communication.** *"Mobile implies wireless so it seems (although there are exceptions to the rules)."*

**Feature 2 — Location Changes Over Time**
- If a device's location changes regularly, so will perhaps the **services locally available** — requiring attention to **dynamically discovering services**, and letting services **announce their presence.**
- Also often need to know **where a device actually is** — actual geographical coordinates (tracking/tracing applications), or simply its **network position** (mobile IP).

#### Communication Under Mobility — MANETs and Disruption-Tolerant Networks

- **Changing locations profoundly affects communication.** Consider a wireless **mobile ad hoc network (MANET)** — even after two devices discover each other's network address, how do we **route messages** between them?
- **Static routes are generally not sustainable**, as nodes along the routing path easily move out of range, **invalidating the path.** For large MANETs, a-priori set-up paths aren't viable.
- These are **disruption-tolerant networks**: connectivity between two nodes cannot simply be guaranteed.

**The Two Principles for Message Passing in Disruption-Tolerant Networks:**

1. **Flooding-based techniques** — a message gradually spreads through part of the network to eventually reach the destination. *(Imposes redundant communication — "the price we have to pay.")*
2. **Intermediate storage** — an intermediate node **stores a received message** until it encounters another node to pass it on. A node becomes a **temporary carrier** of a message.

```
Passing messages in a (mobile) disruption-tolerant network:

Source N1 ──[message passing]──► N2 ──[N2 MOVES]──► N3 ──► Destination
```

- **Selective passing can improve efficiency:** e.g., passing messages only among nodes of the **same class** as source/destination, or only to **well-connected nodes** (those recently in range of many other nodes).

#### Social Awareness and "Pocket Switched Networks"

- With growing interest in **complex social networks** and the explosion of smartphone use, research combines **social behavior analysis** and information dissemination in **pocket switched networks** — networks where **nodes are people** (their mobile devices), and **links form when two people encounter each other**, letting devices exchange data.

**Miklas et al.'s Friends vs. Strangers Study**
- Characterizes two people as either **friends** (interact frequently) or **strangers** (low recurring encounter count).
- **Finding:** if Alice hands the message to **each of her friends**, and each friend passes it to Bob as soon as encountered, the message reaches Bob with a delay exceeding **approximately 10%** of the best-attainable delay. **Any other strategy** (e.g., forwarding to only one or two friends) performs **much worse.** Passing to a **stranger** has **no significant effect.**
- *"It makes a huge difference if nodes take friend relationships into account, but even then it is still necessary to judiciously adopt a forwarding strategy."*

**Community Detection**
- For **large groups**, messages may need to travel between **different communities** — roughly, a community is a group of vertices with **many links between its members** and only few links to vertices in other groups. Unfortunately, many community-detection algorithms require **complete information** on social structure, making them **practically infeasible** for optimizing communication in mobile networks. A few **decentralized solutions** exist (Hui et al.).

**Human Mobility Is Predictable**
- A general observation: people tend to **stay put**, and further analysis shows people tend to **return to the same place** after 24, 48, or 72 hours. **Song et al. show human mobility is actually remarkably well predictable.**

### 15.6 Sensor Networks

> Sensor networks in many cases form part of the **enabling technology** for pervasiveness — many solutions for sensor networks return in pervasive applications.

#### What Makes Them Interesting From a Distributed Systems Perspective

- They are **more than just a collection of input devices** — sensor nodes often **collaborate to efficiently process the sensed data** in an application-specific manner, making them very different from, e.g., traditional computer networks.

#### Basic Characteristics
- Generally consist of **tens to hundreds or thousands** of relatively small nodes, each equipped with one or more **sensing devices.**
- Nodes can often act as **actuators** — typical example: **automatic activation of sprinklers** when a fire has been detected.
- Most use **wireless communication**, and nodes are often **battery powered.** Their **limited resources, restricted communication capabilities, and constrained power consumption** demand that **efficiency be high on the list of design criteria.**

#### Individual Node Architecture and Programming Support

- Conceptually, an individual node doesn't differ much from "normal" computers: above the hardware, a software layer akin to a traditional OS (low-level network access, sensor/actuator access, memory management), plus support for **specific services** (localization, local storage, messaging/routing).
- **For sensor networks**, instead of "middleware," it's better to think of **programming support.**

#### Worked Example — Abstract Regions (Welsh & Mainland)

```c
region = k_nearest_region.create(8);
reading = get_sensor_reading();
region.putvar(reading_key, reading);
max_id = region.reduce(OP_MAXID, reading_key);
```

- **Line 1:** a node creates a **region of its eight nearest neighbors.**
- **Line 2:** it fetches a value from its sensor(s).
- **Line 3:** this reading is written to the previously defined region (keyed by `reading_key`).
- **Line 4:** the node checks **whose** sensor reading in the region was the largest, returned in `max_id`.

#### Sensor Networks as Distributed Databases

- One of **four possible ways** of accessing sensor data (per Mottola & Picco): treating the network as a **distributed database.**
- Common in **measurement and surveillance applications**, where an operator extracts information via queries like *"What is the northbound traffic load on highway 1 at Santa Cruz?"* — resembling traditional database queries, answered via **collaboration of many sensors** along the relevant path, while leaving others untouched.

**Two Extremes for Organizing a Sensor Network Database (Figure 13)**

```
(a) Centralized:
   Sensor network ──[all sensor data sent directly]──► Operator's site

(b) Query-forwarded (each sensor processes/stores):
   Operator's site ──[query]──► Sensor network
   Sensor network ──[only answers sent back]──► Operator's site
```

- **Neither extreme is very attractive:** (a) requires sensors to send **all** measured data through the network — potentially wasting network resources and energy. (b) discards the **aggregation capabilities** of sensors, which would allow much less data to be returned.
- **What's needed:** facilities for **in-network data processing**, similar to the "abstract regions" example above.

#### In-Network Processing — Tree-Based Aggregation (TinyDB)

- One approach: forward a query to all sensor nodes along a **tree** encompassing all nodes, aggregating results as they propagate back to the **root** (where the initiator is). Aggregation happens where **two or more branches come together.**
- **Difficult questions this raises:**
    - How do we **(dynamically) set up an efficient tree**?
    - How does **aggregation** take place — can it be controlled?
    - What happens when **network links fail**?
- **TinyDB** partly addresses these — implements a **declarative (database) interface** to wireless sensor networks, using **any tree-based routing algorithm.** An intermediate node **collects and aggregates** results from its children plus its own findings, sending that toward the root. **Queries span a period of time**, allowing careful scheduling so network resources and energy are optimally consumed.
- **When queries can be initiated from different points**, single-rooted trees (like TinyDB's) may not be efficient enough — sensor networks may instead use **special nodes** where results (and related queries) are forwarded, corresponding directly to the notion of **publish/subscribe systems.**

#### Duty Cycling — Energy Conservation

> Many sensor networks need to operate on an **energy budget** (batteries or other limited power supplies). One approach to reduce consumption: let nodes be **active only part of the time.**

- If a node is active for **T_active** time units, then suspended for **T_suspended**, its **duty cycle τ** is:
  $$
  \tau = \frac{T_{active}}{T_{active} + T_{suspended}}
  $$

- **Typical values:** τ ≈ 10–30%. For networks that must stay operational for **months or years**, values as low as **1%** are critical.

**The Synchronization Problem**
- Nodes generally need to be active **at the same time** for communication to be possible. While suspended, only a node's **local clock continues ticking** — and clocks are subject to **drift**, making waking up at the same time **problematic**, particularly for networks with **very low duty cycles.**
- A group of nodes active at the same time is a **synchronized group.** Two problems:
    1. Ensuring nodes **remain** active at the same time — relatively simple if each node communicates its **current local time**, allowing simple **local clock adjustments.**
    2. **How to merge two different synchronized groups** into one where all nodes are synchronized — **more difficult.** By judiciously sending/reacting to **join messages**, Voulgaris et al. achieved a **highly efficient solution** for networks scaling to **thousands of mobile nodes** while maintaining a duty cycle **below 1%.**

## 16. Outlook — Dependability and Scalability

> The paper closes by focusing on **two areas** that will jointly cover the vast amount of research/development for the coming years: **dependability** and **scalability.**

### 16.1 Dependability — Making Systems Robust and Trustworthy

- As we move into the **digital society**, we become more dependent on distributed systems — increasing the need for systems that can be **justifiably relied upon**: not only appearing to work correctly, but being **shown** to work correctly.
- Many distributed systems are **hidden from sight** — we depend on their correct behavior, but only notice **when they break.** Examples: **critical infrastructures** (electricity, public transportation), electronic banking, online stores, communication.

#### Fault Tolerance vs. Security
- A huge body of knowledge exists on making systems **tolerant to faults** — the key word is **redundancy**, applied in abundance and in many forms.
- **However, more attention is now being paid to protecting against DELIBERATE attacks** — **security is moving more into the forefront** of systems research, a trend expected to continue as distributed systems move into **open environments.**
- **Example of the difficulty:** peer-to-peer systems introduced in the last decade virtually all operate in the **safe environment of a single, protected organization** — Urdaneta et al. survey shows building **open and secure** P2P systems is **virtually impossible.**

#### Privacy Concerns
- With the ubiquity of distributed systems and their **data-processing power**, respecting **privacy and identity** is leading to much debate. *"Technology alone cannot provide the final solutions"* — expect more **blending between distributed-systems technology and research on societal/ethical issues**, along with emphasis on **human-systems interaction.**

### 16.2 Scalability — "The Internet of Everything"

- As connectivity quality/ease grows, so will distributed systems scale up. *"Decades ago we could sensibly speak of a stand-alone computer. This no longer makes any sense"* — all systems we develop are connected to the Internet, and thus to each other.
- With increased connectivity comes **vastly increased data processing** — the more input channels/links, the more data to process. **Much research will focus on scalable solutions; without scalability, a solution will be quickly dismissed.**

#### Realistic Workloads Matter
- Scalability solutions will need testing using **realistic workloads** — already widely adopted, but *"the days of simulations with only synthetic workloads will become less accepted."* At minimum, simulation experiments will need to be **backed up with real-world data.**

#### Distributed Systems as Objects of Study — Complex Dynamical Networked Systems
- Scalability's concentration brings a **new element** into distributed-systems research: viewing these systems as **inherently complex, dynamical networked systems.**
- Increasing focus on **statistical properties** of distributed systems, including for **proving correct or desirable behavior.**
- *"Instead of concentrating only on the internal and architectural elements of a distributed system, much more emphasis will be put on viewing the system as a whole and finding the proper formalisms for describing the observed behavior. A distributed system thus becomes an OBJECT OF STUDY, much like observing and trying to explain natural phenomenon."*
- This trend follows recent research on understanding the **structure and dynamics** of the **Internet and the Web.**

## 17. Glossary of Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Distributed system** | A collection of autonomous computing elements that appears to its users as a single coherent system. |
| **Node** | A computing element, either a hardware device or a software process. |
| **Overlay network** | The (often structured or unstructured) network of neighbor relationships among nodes forming a distributed system. |
| **Open group / Closed group** | An open group allows any node to join; a closed group requires a separate mechanism for admission. |

| **Access transparency** | Hiding differences in data representation and object access methods. |

| **Migration transparency** | Hiding that an object may move to another location, initiated by users. |
| **Replication transparency** | Hiding that several copies of an object exist. |
| **Concurrency transparency** | Hiding that an object may be shared by several independent users. |
| **Failure transparency** | Hiding the failure and (automatic) recovery of an object. |

| **Interoperability** | The extent to which independently-built implementations can co-exist and work together via a common standard. |
| **Portability** | The extent to which an application built for one distributed system can run unmodified on another implementing the same interfaces. |
| **Separation of policy and mechanism** | A design principle where the system provides flexible mechanisms, while policy (how those mechanisms are used) is left configurable. |
| **Scalability** | The ability of a system to grow (in size, geography, or administrative span) without a noticeable loss of performance or manageability. |
| **Size scalability** | Adding more users/resources without noticeable performance loss. |

| **Administrative scalability** | The system can be managed even across many independent administrative organizations. |

| **Peter Deutsch's fallacies** | Eight false assumptions commonly made when first developing distributed applications. |
| **Cluster computing** | High-performance distributed computing over similar, closely-networked machines under one administrative domain. |
| **Grid computing** | High-performance distributed computing as a federation of heterogeneous systems across multiple administrative domains. |
| **Virtual organization** | A federation realizing collaborative access rights to shared grid resources across institutions. |
| **Cloud computing** | An easily usable, accessible, dynamically configurable pool of virtualized resources, typically pay-per-use. |
| **IaaS / PaaS / SaaS** | Infrastructure-, Platform-, and Software-as-a-Service — the three cloud service types. |
| **ACID properties** | Atomic, Consistent, Isolated, Durable — the four characteristic properties of transactions. |
| **Nested transaction** | A transaction composed of subtransactions, possibly run in parallel on different machines. |

| **MOM (Message-Oriented Middleware)** | Middleware allowing applications to communicate via logical contact points/subjects, without requiring simultaneous availability. |
| **Pervasive system** | A distributed system intended to naturally blend into the environment, blurring the user/system boundary. |
| **Ubiquitous computing system** | A pervasive system that is continuously present, with implicit, context-aware, autonomous, intelligent interaction. |
| **Context awareness** | A system's ability to characterize and use information about the situation (who, where, when, what) relevant to interaction. |

| **Pocket switched network** | A network where nodes are people's mobile devices, and links form upon physical encounters. |
| **Duty cycle (τ)** | The fraction of time a sensor node is active, versus suspended, to conserve energy. |
| **In-network processing** | Aggregating/processing sensor data within the network itself, rather than centrally or purely at the edges. |

## 18. Summary Tables

### 18.1 The Seven Types of Distribution Transparency

| Transparency | Hides... | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Access | Data representation & access differences | Unified file-system-like access across heterogeneous machines |
| Location | Where an object is located | A URL with no location clue |
| Relocation | That an object moved while in use | Moving a site between datacenters |
| Migration | User-initiated object mobility | Mobile phone conversations continuing while moving |
| Replication | That several copies exist | Consistent naming across replicas |
| Concurrency | That an object is shared by multiple users | Locking / transactions on a shared database |
| Failure | Failure and automatic recovery | Masking a slow/dead server distinction as best possible |

### 18.2 The Four Design Goals

| Goal | Core Idea |
|---|---|
| Supporting resource sharing | Easy access to remote resources (economics, collaboration, P2P file sharing) |
| Making distribution transparent | Hide physical distribution — but know when NOT to (latency, consistency, location-based services) |
| Being open | Interoperability, portability, extensibility via IDLs; separate policy from mechanism |
| Being scalable | Size, geographical, and administrative scalability, addressed via hiding latency, partition/distribution, and replication |

### 18.3 Scaling Techniques

| Technique | Applies To | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Hiding communication latencies | Geographical scalability | Asynchronous communication, new threads, or moving computation to the client |
| Partitioning and distribution | Size/geographical scalability | Splitting a component into parts, spreading them (e.g., DNS zones, the Web's server distribution) |
| Replication (incl. caching) | Availability & performance | Multiple copies close to clients — at the cost of consistency management (CAP problem) |

### 18.4 Three Types of Distributed Systems

| Type | Subtypes | Key Distinguishing Trait |
|---|---|---|
| Distributed computing systems | Cluster, Grid, Cloud | High-performance computing; homogeneous (cluster) to heterogeneous/federated (grid) to virtualized/pay-per-use (cloud) |
| Distributed information systems | Transaction processing, EAI | Focus on integrating networked applications, transactions, ACID properties |
| Pervasive systems | Ubiquitous, Mobile, Sensor networks | Naturally distributed; blurred user/system boundary; resource-constrained, wireless, context-aware |

### 18.5 Cluster vs. Grid vs. Cloud Computing

| | Cluster | Grid | Cloud |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hardware homogeneity | High (similar machines, same OS) | Low (federated, heterogeneous) | Virtualized (abstracted away) |
| Administrative domain | Single | Multiple (federation, virtual organizations) | Vendor-managed |
| Primary purpose | Parallel programming / HPC | Collaborative resource sharing (virtual organizations) | Dynamically configurable, pay-per-use resource pool |
| Example | Beowulf clusters, MOSIX | WLCG, TeraGrid | AWS EC2/S3, Google App Engine, MS Azure |

### 18.6 The Four EAI Integration Approaches

| Approach | Simplicity | Main Drawback |
|---|---|---|
| File transfer | Very simple | Format/layout, file management, update propagation agreements needed |
| Shared database | Easy notification via triggers | Common schema needed; can become a bottleneck |
| RPC | Encapsulates action sequences cleanly | Requires caller & callee simultaneously available |
| Messaging | Doesn't require simultaneity | Formatting, addressing, and lost-message handling still needed |

## 21. Big-Picture Takeaways

1. **The paper deliberately offers a "loose" rather than precise definition of distributed systems** — because decades of literature have failed to converge on a single satisfactory one — grounding the field instead in two practical characteristics: autonomous collaborating nodes, and the appearance of a single coherent system to users.
2. **Coherence, not perfect transparency, is the real design target.** The paper is explicit that a "single-system view" hiding literally everything is "asking too much" — the more honest, achievable goal is a system that behaves according to user expectations, which sometimes means deliberately EXPOSING distribution (location-based services) rather than hiding it.
3. **Partial failure is the defining, inescapable difficulty of distributed systems** — Lamport's quote crystallizes why this differs fundamentally from single-machine software: the failure of a component you didn't even know existed can break your own work, and no amount of engineering makes this problem disappear, only more gracefully managed.
4. **Middleware exists to spare every application from re-solving the same distributed-systems problems** — RPC, transactions, service composition, and reliability guarantees (like Horus's atomic message delivery) are recurring needs across countless applications, and middleware's entire value proposition is turning these into reusable, shared infrastructure.
5. **The four design goals (resource sharing, transparency, openness, scalability) are in genuine tension with each other and with performance** — the paper repeatedly shows that pursuing one goal too aggressively (e.g., full transparency, or strict policy/mechanism separation) creates real costs elsewhere (surprisingly high latency, exploding configuration complexity), meaning distributed systems design is inherently a balancing act, not a checklist.
6. **Scalability is not one problem but three genuinely different ones** — size scalability is "the least problematic, technically" (often just buy more capacity), geographical scalability runs into hard physical limits (the speed of light, unreliable/limited-bandwidth WANs), and administrative scalability is the hardest because it's fundamentally a human and political problem, not a technical one, illustrated starkly by how effortlessly peer-to-peer networks sidestepped it by putting end users in control.
7. **The CAP theorem is the mathematical wall behind why "just replicate everything" doesn't solve scalability for free** — replication buys availability and performance, but strong consistency across replicas requires global synchronization that is inherently hard to scale, since network latency has a physical lower bound.
8. **Peter Deutsch's eight fallacies remain the single most quotable, practical warning in the paper** — every one of them maps directly to a property (reliability, security, heterogeneity, topology, latency, bandwidth, cost, administration) that simply doesn't exist as an issue in non-distributed software, explaining why so many first attempts at distributed applications end up needing to be patched after painful discovery.
9. **The three system-type categories (computing, information, pervasive) trace a historical arc from raw computational power, through enterprise integration, to systems that vanish into the environment** — and each category's hardest problems are shaped by what it optimizes for: cluster/grid/cloud computing by performance and resource federation, information systems by transactional correctness and integration, and pervasive systems by resource constraints, mobility, and blurred human-computer boundaries.
10. **The paper's own outlook — dependability and scalability as the two dominant future research threads — reframes distributed systems as objects of scientific study in their own right**, akin to natural phenomena, where statistical and network-science tools (not just architectural engineering) become necessary to understand and predict behavior at Internet scale.
