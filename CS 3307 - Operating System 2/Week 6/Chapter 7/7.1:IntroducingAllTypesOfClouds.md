## 1. Introduction — Why So Many Cloud Models Exist

### 1.1 The Evolution of Cloud Models
- As cloud technologies have **evolved**, **many different cloud models have emerged** to **solve specific business challenges.**
- **This evolution isn't surprising**, since **more and more businesses** view the cloud as the **future of their computing environment.**

### 1.2 Complexity With a Purpose
- Although these **different computing models** make the cloud world **more complicated**, this complexity has **given rise to cloud solutions** that incorporate **ALL the capabilities needed** to **efficiently and effectively solve specific problems.**

### 1.3 More Types ≠ Conflict

> **Having more types of clouds doesn't mean they're in conflict — quite the opposite.**

- **When a business builds a technical strategy** for using **many different cloud environments** in a **harmonized and coordinated way**, this is called a **MULTICLOUD.**

*(Note: this chapter's covered material focuses specifically on the "Public Clouds" category — commercial, open community [social and technical], and government clouds — as the foundational building block before other cloud models like private, hybrid, and multicloud are introduced.)*

## 2. Understanding Public Clouds — Overview

### 2.1 Why Public Clouds Are the Most Well-Known

> **Public clouds are probably the MOST well-known type of cloud**, particularly because they were the **FIRST viable cloud computing environments for consumers.**

### 2.2 A Broad Selection of Services

- Because of the **various classes of users**, public clouds offer a **BROAD selection of computing services.**
- **Range of services:** from **SaaS applications** to the **infrastructure services** used to **build applications** — public clouds must be **prepared for their users to use OR build ANY kind of applications.**

### 2.3 The Service Model Spectrum

| Service Model | What's Provided |
|---|---|
| **IaaS (Infrastructure as a Service)** | **ALL major public clouds** offer this — users **select an operating system** and **deploy applications.** |
| **PaaS (Platform as a Service)** | **Many public clouds** also offer this — the **operating system is HIDDEN**, and deployed applications use **ONLY PaaS services.** |
| **Serverless** | Some PaaS services now offer **serverless environments**, where the **cloud takes on EVEN MORE responsibility** for the **underlying services** used by applications. |

```
        IaaS                    PaaS                  Serverless
   (User manages OS       (OS hidden; app       (Cloud manages even
    + deploys apps)        uses PaaS services      MORE underlying
                            only)                    responsibility)
        │                       │                          │
        └───────── Increasing cloud provider responsibility ──────►
```

### 2.4 The Three Types of Public Clouds Covered

1. **Commercial public clouds.**
2. **Open community clouds.**
3. **Public clouds designed for use by the U.S. government.**

## 3. Commercial Public Clouds

### 3.1 Primary Purpose and Accessibility

- **Commercial public clouds** are **primarily intended for professional and business use**, although they're **available to ANYONE with a web browser.**
- **What they offer:** services that provide **value to businesses** — such as:
    - **SaaS applications.**
    - **Infrastructure services**, such as **databases and scalable storage.**

### 3.2 Security Level

> Because a **professional service** is being offered, commercial public clouds provide a **HIGH level of security and protection.**

### 3.3 How You Access Them

- **Typical access method:** via a **portal on the web**, to **deploy software** or **use computing/storage services.**
- **For SaaS applications:** even **easier** — just go to the **application's address on the web.**

## 4. Service Level Agreements (SLAs) in Depth

### 4.1 Definition

> An **SLA** is **"an agreement outlining the performance and operational obligations of the cloud provider to the consumer of a service."**

### 4.2 Who the SLA Really Protects

> **An SLA is typically designed to PROTECT THE VENDOR rather than the customer.**

- **However:** vendors have an **UNWRITTEN commitment** to **maintain the level of security and service required** to **protect their BUSINESS RELATIONSHIPS** — even beyond the strict letter of the SLA.

### 4.3 What SLAs Typically Address

1. **Meantime Between Failures (MTBF)** and **Meantime To Repair (MTTR).**
2. **Service availability.**
3. **Performance and response time.**
4. **Security commitments.**
5. **Data protection commitments.**
6. **Customer notification method** in the event of an **outage.**
7. **Exit strategies** ensuring a **smooth transition** if the customer must **terminate service.**

### 4.4 The Fine-Print Warning

> **Although the SLA may appear to give customers a great deal of comfort... as with any contract, the FINE PRINT matters.**

- **SLAs describe situations where the cloud provider is NOT responsible for an outage.**
- **Real-world example:** many **wide-ranging cloud outages** have **NOT triggered SLA clauses**, because the outage was caused by **networking problems** or **other issues** that the agreement **specifically identified** as excluded.

```
Cloud outage occurs
       │
       ▼
Was it caused by something the SLA specifically EXCLUDES
(e.g., networking issues explicitly carved out)?
       │
   ┌───┴───┐
  YES        NO
   │          │
   ▼          ▼
SLA clause  SLA clause
NOT          IS triggered —
triggered   provider owes
(customer   remediation/
has no      compensation
recourse)
```

## 5. Pricing Models — Infrastructure vs. SaaS

### 5.1 Infrastructure/Storage — Pay-As-You-Use

> **A pay-as-you-use cost model is fairly standard for cloud infrastructure and storage.**

- **As you scale UP** (more users, larger applications) → **costs INCREASE.**
- **As you scale DOWN** (lower usage times) → this **metering cost model helps you SAVE money**, if your environment is designed to **scale down accordingly.**
- **Billing:** typically **monthly.**

### 5.2 SaaS — Vendor-Defined Pricing Models

- **SaaS applications have their OWN pricing models**, defined **by the SaaS vendor.**
- **Worked example — CRM SaaS (e.g., Salesforce):** you must **sign a contract** under its **terms of service.**
    - **Contract term:** may be as short as **a month**, or (more typically) **a year or longer.**
- **License transfer possibility:** in some cases, you can **TRANSFER an on-premises license** you already hold, for **usage in the cloud.**
- **Pay-when-you-use example (e.g., PayPal):** you **sign a contract**, but **only pay when you USE the service.**
- **Freemium possibility:** sometimes a SaaS application is **available for FREE**, but with **LIMITED functionality.**

### 5.3 Flexibility Without Long-Term Commitment

> **When SaaS applications aren't purchased with a long-term commitment, users can DROP their use of the application WHENEVER they want.**

- **This freedom is ESPECIALLY relevant** during the **early stage of the relationship.**
- **Common pattern:** a customer signs on for a **month-long trial subscription.**
    - **If service is poor** or **not a good match**: the customer will **inevitably look elsewhere.**
- **Caveat:** the ability to **easily sever a relationship** also **depends on HOW MUCH integration and customization** the customer has already done (deep integration = harder to leave).

### 5.4 Data Management Responsibility

- **SaaS applications typically MANAGE customer data**, which **adds to the level of responsibility** the SaaS vendor must provide for **securing and protecting information.**

## 6. Data and IP Ownership Concerns

### 6.1 Who Should Own What

> **Although the SaaS vendor MANAGES the data, the data SHOULD BE OWNED by the customer.**

- **Similarly:** customers who **build software** in a **SaaS or PaaS environment** should **OWN the software intellectual property** they've created.

### 6.2 Practical Recommendation

> **It is important that customers maintain a COPY of their proprietary business logic AS WELL AS their own data.**

- **Critical warning:** *"Be sure to read the fine print; there are environments where vendors CLAIM the rights to customers' data or intellectual property."*

## 7. Open Social Community Clouds

### 7.1 Defining "Open Community Cloud"

> **The most OPEN type of cloud environment is an open community cloud** — a cloud environment that **doesn't require any criteria for joining** other than **signing up and creating a password.**

- **Anonymity possible:** you can **even create an account under an ALIAS.**
- **The word "open"** alludes to:
    1. A cloud that **isn't commercial.**
    2. Often, also refers to **open source** — the **nonproprietary software** upon which **most major clouds are built.**

### 7.2 Two Primary Types of Open Social Community Clouds

#### Type 1 — Commercial Sites With a Strong Advertising Model

- **Examples:** **Facebook, LinkedIn, Twitter** — sites with **social networking connections.**
- **May be:** **private or publicly owned.**
- **Revenue model:** these sites **RARELY charge a fee to users** — instead, they use the **SIZE AND SCOPE of their user base** to **sell advertising.**
- **Exception — premium tiers:** some sites (e.g., **LinkedIn**) have **professional, fee-based services** offering access to **more in-depth services/information.**
- **Who the "real" client is:**
  > Although these sites do **NOT** have an **explicit guarantee of service to USERS**, they DO have an **obligation to ADVERTISERS.**
    - **Key implication:** *"These sites view ADVERTISERS, not individual users, as their clients"* — so **you shouldn't rely on these companies to COMPLETELY safeguard your data.**
    - **Additional risk:** they may **sell SOME of your data** (anonymized or not) to **various other companies**, for the purpose of **displaying highly targeted advertising.**

#### Type 2 — Open Community Sites With a Compelling Focus

- **Purpose:** enable **individuals with common interests** to **participate in online discussions.**
- **Examples:**
    - **Professionals in a certain industry** (e.g., **manufacturing or retail**) sharing ideas.
    - **Hobby-based communities** (e.g., **biking or chess**).
- **Security level:** **typically VERY LITTLE security**, and **NO guarantee** the site will **remain active over time.**
- **A real risk:** *"Open community sites can DISAPPEAR when the most active members MOVE ON."*

### 7.3 Common Characteristics Across All Open Social Community Sites

1. **A relatively simple sign-up process** — all you need is a **login name, password, and an email account.**
2. **Requests for additional information** — **more sophisticated sites** may ask for information about yourself; **others don't ask AT ALL.**
3. **Low-level security** — *"With little effort, someone WITHOUT authorization... could gain access to an account."*
    - **However:** nowadays, these sites include **disclaimers** about **how they will/won't use customer data**, explain their **responsibilities** in managing the site, and **warn users** not to use copyrighted material, etc.
4. **NO service-level guarantee to the individual user.**
    - **Important nuance:** the **lack of a guarantee** does **NOT** mean these sites perform poorly. **Performance depends on:**
        - The underlying **technology.**
        - **How important** that site is to those **managing** it.
    - **Example — Facebook:** it's **CRITICAL** to Facebook's business that its site is **ALWAYS available** for mobile/web users.
    - **Contrast — niche sites:** a **niche hobby/trade-group site** may experience **outages from time to time**, since it uses the **LEAST expensive hosting services available.**

### 7.4 The Risk of Abandonment / Shutdown

- **If the company changes its business focus**, or determines the **community is no longer worth the expense**, it **can SHUT DOWN the community AT WILL.**
- **NO guarantees** are made that content **generated and stored** on the community cloud will remain **accessible for ANY specific amount of time.**
- **Consequence:** community members who've become **dependent on the site** **can do NOTHING** about it.
- **Abandoned community sites are a COMMON occurrence** — e.g., both **Google** and **LinkedIn** allow users to create **their own online communities** on any topic:
    - **Some** are well-moderated/managed → **hundreds or thousands of visitors.**
    - **Others** become **phantom sites** and **disappear.**

### 7.5 The Absence of SLAs

> **Keep in mind that these open social community clouds do NOT have SLAs.**

- **The risk this creates:** individuals AND small/medium businesses may **upload IMPORTANT information** onto these community clouds **without understanding the potential risk.**
- **Example:** if **photos, videos, or documents** are **deleted or otherwise lost**, you will have **NO recourse.**
- **Support reality:** in many cases, even **reaching a live person for help** will be **difficult.**

### 7.6 A More Sophisticated Exception

- **Some community sites are VERY sophisticated.**
- **Sites with a STRONG revenue model** based on **selling advertising to users** have a **compelling incentive** to **ensure a minimum amount of downtime**, since downtime **impacts REVENUE.**
- **A subtlety:** some of these communities may **ACTUALLY BE private clouds** that are **open only to QUALIFIED customers** (i.e., not truly "open" despite appearances).

### 7.7 Distinguishing Community Clouds From Regular Websites

> When you use one of these sites, it may be **hard to tell** whether you're using a **cloud site** or just a **regular website.**

- **The distinguishing test:** *"If the site offers a way for users to CONSTRUCT applications using CONFIGURABLE computing resources, then you can be sure you're using a community cloud."*
- **Note:** there's **nothing wrong with regular websites** — but they **typically offer users LESS ability** to **perform processing** on their data and other functionality **than community clouds.**

## 8. Open Technical Community Clouds

### 8.1 A Second Type of Open Community Cloud

> There is **another type** of open community cloud — a **TECHNICAL INCUBATOR cloud** that serves as a **platform for JOINT DEVELOPMENT** of, mainly, **open-source systems.**

- **Why open source matters here:** being based on open source helps these clouds stay **NONPROPRIETARY and NONCOMMERCIAL.**

### 8.2 Example 1 — GitHub

- **What it is:** a **web-based hosting service** for **version control** of software systems.
- **Scope of use:** used for a **very broad range** of software development projects, by **software developers around the world.**

### 8.3 Example 2 — Mass Open Cloud

- **Name origin:** "Mass" is short for **Massachusetts**, where it is **located.**
- **How it was built:** using **donations from software and hardware vendors**, and **nurtured by a variety of nonprofit and educational institutions.**
- **The goal of this cloud:** to serve as a **self-sustaining, at-scale, PUBLIC cloud** used by **researchers and industry** to:
    - **Innovate important new technologies.**
    - **Involve the public** in its innovations.

### 8.4 Summary — Social vs. Technical Open Community Clouds

| | Open Social Community Clouds | Open Technical Community Clouds |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary purpose** | Social connection, shared-interest discussion | Joint software development, mainly open-source |
| **Examples** | Facebook, LinkedIn, Twitter, hobby/trade forums | GitHub, Mass Open Cloud |
| **Revenue model (if any)** | Advertising (users are NOT the "client") | Often donation-based / nonprofit-nurtured |
| **Security/guarantees** | Low security, no SLA, can vanish at will | Varies, but oriented around open, collaborative development |

## 9. Government Clouds

### 9.1 Scope and Accessibility

> You may have read about clouds created **specifically for use by the United States government.**

- **Accessibility:** *"Unless you work for the government, you'll probably NEVER have access to these clouds"* — so this form of cloud isn't discussed in great detail in the source material.

### 9.2 Why the Government Needs Cloud Technology

- **Not surprisingly**, the U.S. government needs the **power and flexibility** of the cloud for its **many computing systems** (used and developed).
- **The public cloud has many of the KINDS of services** the government needs.
- **Shared organizational needs:** like businesses, government organizations need to **SHARE information** between **different groups.**
- **Big data relevance:** **big data and data science** are **critical for certain governmental organizations.**
- **Concrete example — law enforcement:** agencies such as the **FBI and CIA** utilize the cloud because of its **ability to provide a big data and analytics platform.**

### 9.3 Why Public Clouds Aren't Always Sufficient

> **However... the government has SPECIAL needs that AREN'T served by public clouds.**

- **Example:** although public clouds have **EXCELLENT security characteristics and services**, the government has **MORE STRINGENT security requirements** than the **public sector.**

### 9.4 The Stakes Are Categorically Different

> **Although private companies can face massive fines, embarrassment, and loss of trust from a data breach, a GOVERNMENTAL breach can lead to an INTERNATIONAL INCIDENT.**

- **Key contrast:** a private-sector breach is a **business problem** (financial, reputational); a government breach can escalate into a **geopolitical/diplomatic problem** — a fundamentally higher order of consequence.

### 9.5 The No-Shared-Infrastructure Requirement

- **One of the government's core security requirements:** they **may NOT share ANY infrastructure with the public** — this includes:
    - **Servers**
    - **Storage**
    - **Networking**
    - **Power**
- **An even stricter variant:** **many government organizations REQUIRE** that their data **NEVER travel through ANY shared infrastructure** at all — not even transiently.

```
Public Cloud (shared infrastructure):
   [Server]--[Storage]--[Network]--[Power]
       shared across MANY different customers/tenants

Government Cloud (segregated infrastructure):
   [Server]--[Storage]--[Network]--[Power]
       DEDICATED -- NOT shared with public/commercial customers
       (data must never even TRANSIT shared infrastructure)
```

### 9.6 How Government Clouds Are Actually Built

> **Government clouds are generally created by PUBLIC cloud providers who set up SEGREGATED versions of their public clouds.**

- **What carries over:** **many of the services found in the public cloud** can ALSO be found in the **government version.**
- **What doesn't carry over:** **SOME services may be EXCLUDED** from the government version.

### 9.7 Marketplace Restrictions — Third-Party Software

- **Public clouds generally include MARKETPLACES**, where **third parties** make their **applications and services available.**
- **In government clouds, this is much more restricted:**
  > **Third-party software is NOT made available in a government cloud WITHOUT passing RIGOROUS REVIEWS** to ensure the software is **safe for use** in the government cloud.

```
Public Cloud Marketplace:
  Third-party app --> Listed/available (minimal gatekeeping)

Government Cloud Marketplace:
  Third-party app --> RIGOROUS REVIEW process --> Approved? --> Made available
                                                 \-> Rejected --> Excluded entirely
```

### 9.8 Summary — Public Cloud vs. Government Cloud

| | Public Cloud | Government Cloud |
|---|---|---|
| **Infrastructure sharing** | Shared across many customers/tenants | NOT shared — segregated, dedicated infrastructure |
| **Breach consequences** | Fines, embarrassment, loss of trust (business-level) | Potential INTERNATIONAL INCIDENT (geopolitical-level) |
| **Underlying provider** | The cloud provider itself | Same public cloud providers, but running a segregated version |
| **Service availability** | Full service catalog | Some services excluded |
| **Third-party marketplace apps** | Generally available with minimal gatekeeping | Only available after rigorous security review |
| **Data transit** | May traverse shared infrastructure | Often required to NEVER traverse shared infrastructure |

*(This section remains intentionally brief in the source material, since access to government clouds is typically restricted to government employees/contractors.)*

## 10. Glossary of Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Multicloud** | A harmonized, coordinated technical strategy for using many different cloud environments together. |
| **Public cloud** | A cloud environment offering a broad selection of computing services, accessible to consumers and businesses generally. |
| **IaaS (Infrastructure as a Service)** | A cloud service model where users select an OS and deploy applications, managing infrastructure directly. |
| **PaaS (Platform as a Service)** | A cloud service model where the OS is hidden, and applications use only platform-provided services. |
| **Serverless** | A PaaS variant where the cloud provider takes on even more responsibility for underlying services. |
| **Commercial public cloud** | A professionally/business-oriented public cloud offering SaaS and infrastructure services with high security. |
| **Service Level Agreement (SLA)** | A contract outlining the performance/operational obligations of a cloud provider to a service consumer. |
| **MTBF (Meantime Between Failures)** | An SLA metric describing the average time between service failures. |
| **MTTR (Meantime To Repair)** | An SLA metric describing the average time needed to repair/restore service after a failure. |
| **Pay-as-you-use** | A cost model where charges scale with actual infrastructure/storage usage. |
| **Open community cloud** | A cloud environment requiring no criteria for joining beyond signing up and creating a password. |
| **Open social community cloud** | An open community cloud oriented around social connection or shared-interest discussion (e.g., Facebook, hobby forums). |
| **Open technical community cloud** | An open community cloud serving as a platform for joint, typically open-source, software development (e.g., GitHub). |
| **Government cloud** | A cloud environment created specifically for use by a government (e.g., the U.S. government), with more stringent security requirements. |
| **Segregated infrastructure** | Dedicated servers, storage, networking, and power that are NOT shared with public/commercial cloud customers, as required by many government cloud policies. |
| **Marketplace (cloud)** | A public cloud feature where third parties make applications/services available; in government clouds, entry requires passing rigorous security review. |

## 11. Summary Tables

### 11.1 The Three Types of Public Clouds

| Type | Primary Audience | Key Characteristic |
|---|---|---|
| Commercial public clouds | Professional/business users (but accessible to anyone) | High security, SLA-backed, pay-as-you-use or vendor-defined SaaS pricing |

| Area | What It Covers |
|---|---|
| MTBF / MTTR | Failure frequency and repair time |
| Service availability | Uptime guarantees |
| Performance and response time | Speed/latency commitments |
| Security commitments | Protection standards |
| Data protection commitments | How customer data is safeguarded |
| Customer notification method | How outages are communicated |
| Exit strategies | Smooth transition if service is terminated |

### 11.3 Cloud Infrastructure/Storage vs. SaaS Pricing

| | Infrastructure/Storage | SaaS |
|---|---|---|
| Pricing model | Pay-as-you-use (metered) | Vendor-defined (contract, subscription, freemium, pay-per-use) |
| Billing frequency | Typically monthly | Varies (monthly, yearly, or longer) |
| Scaling cost behavior | Costs rise/fall with usage | Depends on vendor's specific pricing structure |

### 11.4 Open Social Community Cloud — Two Sub-Types

| | Commercial (Ad-Supported) | Focus-Based Community |
|---|---|---|

### 11.5 Common Characteristics of Open Social Community Sites

| Characteristic | Detail |
|---|---|
| Sign-up process | Simple — login name, password, email |
| Additional info requests | Varies by site sophistication |
| Security level | Low |
| Service-level guarantee | None to individual users |

## 12. Big-Picture Takeaways

1. **The proliferation of cloud types isn't a sign of a fragmented, conflicted market — it's a natural response to genuinely different business needs**, and multicloud strategies exist precisely to harmonize this diversity rather than fight it.
2. **Public clouds span a service-responsibility spectrum from IaaS through PaaS to serverless**, with each step handing more underlying responsibility to the cloud provider — a business or developer's choice among these reflects how much infrastructure management they want to own versus offload.
3. **SLAs are a customer-protection tool that's actually designed to protect the vendor first** — the fine print matters enormously, since many real-world outages fall into explicitly-carved-out exclusion categories, meaning an SLA's psychological comfort can exceed its actual practical guarantee.
4. **SaaS and infrastructure pricing models diverge sharply**: infrastructure follows a predictable, usage-metered model, while SaaS pricing is essentially whatever the vendor decides, ranging from free-with-limits to long-term contracts — meaning customers must actively read and negotiate SaaS terms rather than assume infrastructure-style transparency.
5. **Data and IP ownership is a genuine, easily-overlooked risk in cloud relationships** — customers should always maintain their own copies of data and proprietary logic, since managing data is not the same as owning it, and some vendor agreements blur or claim that line.
6. **Open community clouds trade away security, guarantees, and longevity in exchange for near-zero barriers to entry** — and the "who is the client" question (advertisers, not users, for ad-supported sites) is the key lens for understanding why these platforms behave the way they do toward the people using them.
7. **The complete absence of SLAs in open community clouds is the single most important practical warning in this material** — users and small businesses who treat these platforms as reliable, permanent storage for important content are taking on real, uncompensated risk, with essentially no recourse if data disappears or the service shuts down.
8. **Open technical community clouds (GitHub, Mass Open Cloud) represent a genuinely different philosophy from social community clouds** — oriented around open-source collaboration and nonprofit/donation-based sustainability rather than advertising revenue, even though both fall under the broader "open community cloud" umbrella.
9. **Government clouds exist because public clouds, despite excellent security, cannot fully satisfy the government's more stringent security requirements** — illustrating that "good enough security for the public sector" and "good enough security for government use" are meaningfully different bars to clear.
