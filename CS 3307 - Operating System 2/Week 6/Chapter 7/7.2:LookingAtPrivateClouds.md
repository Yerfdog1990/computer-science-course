## 1. Introduction — What Motivates a Private Cloud

### 1.1 The Basic Scenario

> **In some situations, a company will want to create a cloud environment that sits BEHIND an on-premises firewall.**

- This is the fundamental motivating scenario for **private clouds** — keeping the cloud environment **inside the company's own security perimeter**, rather than out in the open public cloud.

---

## 2. Private Cloud vs. Data Center vs. Public Cloud

### 2.1 Not Just "A Data Center"

> **Unlike a data center, a private cloud is a POOL OF COMMON RESOURCES optimized for the use of the IT organization.**

- This is a **crucial distinction**: a traditional data center is just a collection of physical machines/racks; a **private cloud** goes further — it's a **pooled, optimized resource** specifically tailored to how the IT organization actually works.

### 2.2 When Building a Private Cloud Makes Sense

> **When an organization regularly builds and deploys complex applications, creating a HIGHLY AUTOMATED private cloud that supports internal needs is practical.**

- **Key trigger condition:** **regular** building/deployment of **complex applications** — this repeated, complex workload is what justifies the investment in automation.

### 2.3 Private Cloud vs. Public Cloud — The Compliance Difference

> **Unlike a public cloud, a private cloud ADHERES TO the company's security, governance, and compliance requirements.**

- **Whatever service level is required for the company APPLIES to the private cloud.**
- **In other words:** the private cloud is built to match the company's own internal standards, rather than the cloud provider's generic, one-size-fits-all standards.

```
Public Cloud:                          Private Cloud:
Generic security/governance/           Security/governance/compliance
compliance standards set BY THE        standards set BY THE COMPANY
CLOUD PROVIDER, applied to all         ITSELF, matching whatever service
customers uniformly                    level the company actually requires
```

## 3. The Three Types of Private Clouds

The source material identifies **three distinct types** of private clouds:

1. **A private cloud owned and managed BY A COMPANY**, for the benefit of its **employees and partners.**
2. **An appliance** that is **based on the services offered in the public cloud**, **managed by a cloud vendor**, but positioned **behind a client's firewall.**
3. **A commercial private cloud**, **operated by a vendor**, to support a company that **doesn't want to build or operate its own** private cloud.

## 4. Privately Owned and Managed Clouds

### 4.1 The Business Model That Creates This Need

> **Organizations with a business model that requires the company to provide COMMERCIAL technology services WITHIN a business ecosystem often will build a private PARTNER cloud** — in which case these companies **effectively BECOME a cloud provider.**

### 4.2 Who Takes on the Responsibility

- **Instead of** hiring a **public cloud vendor** to take **full responsibility** for **creating, managing, monitoring, and updating** the software:
    - **The private cloud provider (the company itself) ASSUMES those responsibilities.**

### 4.3 What This Looks Like in Practice

> **Like commercial public cloud providers, a private partner cloud provider creates an OPTIMIZED environment to support the workloads used by its customers.**

- **The main difference:** in a **private partner cloud scenario**, the company **isn't hiring a THIRD PARTY** to operate the cloud — it's doing it **itself.**

### 4.4 Worked Example — A Financial Services Company

- **Scenario:** a **financial services company** provides **individual banks** with **payment services.**
- **What it can create:** a **sophisticated service** that is **automated and streamlined** to **support its customers** (the individual banks).

#### Why This Company's Private Cloud Is Especially Rigorous

> **Because of the security and compliance demands of its customers**, the private cloud provided by the financial services company is based on a **STRINGENT SLA**, and **even INDEMNIFIES customers against lawsuits.**

- **Why this level of rigor makes economic sense:**
  > Because the financial services company **already acts LIKE an IT vendor** to its customers, it has the **ECONOMIES OF SCALE** to make **creating and managing** such a service **cost-effective.**

```
Financial Services Company
        │
        │ (already provides payment services to many banks —
        │  effectively already acting as an IT vendor)
        ▼
Builds its OWN private partner cloud
        │
        ▼
Offers: stringent SLA + indemnification against lawsuits
        │
        ▼
Economically viable BECAUSE of existing economies of scale
(the company was already positioned to do this at scale)
```

## 5. Appliances Based on Public Cloud Offerings

### 5.1 The Emerging Approach

> **An emerging approach to create an on-premises private cloud is to use an APPLIANCE that is CUSTOM BUILT to re-create the functionality of a public cloud.**

### 5.2 The Core Benefit

> **The benefit of this approach is that TECHNICAL TEAMS will be able to develop, deploy, and maintain applications on the environment that they're FAMILIAR WITH**, while **at the same time keeping certain workloads BEHIND THE FIREWALL.**

- **Key insight:** this approach lets teams keep using the **SAME tools and environment** they already know (from the public cloud), while satisfying the need to **keep sensitive workloads on-premises.**

### 5.3 Why Businesses Choose This Path

> **Many businesses are choosing to keep certain workloads and data ON PREMISES and BEHIND THE FIREWALL because of SECURITY, COMPLIANCE, and REGULATORY concerns.**

### 5.4 Two Sub-Approaches to Appliance-Based Private Clouds

#### Sub-Approach A — Fully Installed/Maintained/Upgraded by the Public Cloud Vendor Itself

> **In some instances, these custom-built and integrated hardware offerings are FULLY INSTALLED, MAINTAINED, and UPGRADED by a public cloud vendor.**

**Worked Example — AWS Outposts**

- **What it offers:** customers get **AWS compute and storage hardware ON PREMISES.**
- **Who manages it:** **AWS itself** — fully installed, maintained, and upgraded by the vendor.

#### Sub-Approach B — Public Cloud Vendors Partnering With Hardware Vendors

> **An alternative approach is for public cloud vendors to PARTNER WITH hardware vendors.**

**Worked Example — Google Cloud Platform (GCP) + HPE**

- **What it offers:** **GCP** partners with hardware vendors like **HPE**, to offer **on-premises instances of Google Cloud.**
- **Key difference from AWS Outposts:** in this model, **public cloud customers CHOOSE their own hardware vendor**, and can **NEGOTIATE the maintenance and licensing contracts** themselves.

#### Additional Industry Example

> **Other cloud providers, such as Microsoft, are also offering ON-PREMISES versions of their cloud services.**

### 5.5 Comparison — The Two Appliance Sub-Approaches

| | Vendor-Fully-Managed (e.g., AWS Outposts) | Vendor-Partners-With-Hardware-Vendor (e.g., GCP + HPE) |
|---|---|---|
| **Who installs/maintains/upgrades the hardware?** | The public cloud vendor itself (AWS) | The partner hardware vendor (e.g., HPE) |
| **Customer's hardware choice** | Fixed — provided directly by AWS | Customer CHOOSES their own hardware vendor |
| **Maintenance/licensing negotiation** | Handled by the cloud vendor | Customer negotiates directly |
| **Example** | AWS Outposts | GCP + HPE on-premises instances |

## 6. Commercial Private Cloud

### 6.1 The Motivating Tension

> **Although many companies see the benefits of creating their OWN private clouds, OTHERS want to have the security and governance of a private cloud but want to OBTAIN that service FROM A THIRD PARTY.**

- **The industry's response:** *"vendors have created private clouds that are based on a CONTRACTED service model."*

### 6.2 How This Differs From a Regular Public Cloud

> **Unlike a commercial public cloud where ANYONE with a credit card can sign up, a private customer contractual cloud REQUIRES that customers sign a FORMAL CONTRACT with the vendor.**

### 6.3 What the Customer Gets in Exchange

- **In exchange (for signing a formal contract), the vendor will provide:**
    1. **Strict governance and security capabilities.**
    2. **Even INDEMNIFY customers from lawsuits** related to the use of the service.

### 6.4 Additional Reliability Features

> **In addition, vendors of commercial open clouds often offer customers PRIVATE cloud options**, including:
- **Virtual private networks.**
- **Mirrored sites** — so that if **ONE data center is offline**, a **SECOND data center can support customers.**

```
Data Center 1 (PRIMARY)  ──mirrored──►  Data Center 2 (BACKUP)
        │
        ▼ (goes offline)
        X
        │
Data Center 2 automatically takes over supporting customers
```

### 6.5 The SLA Payoff

> **As a result of these types of offerings, customers receive a MORE EXPLICIT SLA.**

### 6.6 How This Differs From Traditional Hosting

> **This service is DIFFERENT from hosting because, in the private contractual cloud, the service is designed with a SELF-SERVICE PORTAL INTERFACE**, so that customers can **ADD and SUBTRACT services based on DEMAND.**

- **Key distinguishing feature:** the **self-service, elastic** nature of the offering — this is what separates it from old-style, static hosting arrangements.

### 6.7 The Historical Evolution of Private Cloud Deployment

> **When private clouds first emerged, they required EXPERTS to deploy and configure.**

- **Since then:** *"private clouds have matured, and deployment has become LARGELY AUTOMATED."*

### 6.8 "Cloud in a Box" — Physical Appliances

> **Increasingly, public cloud companies have seen an opportunity to create PHYSICAL cloud computing appliances that can be PLUGGED INTO the data center infrastructure and start working QUICKLY.**

- **Sometimes referred to as: "Cloud in a Box."**

#### The Key Portability Benefit

> **These private clouds INHERIT the services and applications available in the vendor's public cloud**, meaning that **applications, services, and practices can be MOVED SEAMLESSLY** between a vendor's **public cloud** and its **private cloud appliance.**

```
Vendor's Public Cloud                Vendor's Private Cloud Appliance
(services, applications)   ◄────►    ("Cloud in a Box" — plugged in,
                                       inherits the SAME services)
        │                                        │
        └──── Applications/services/practices ────┘
              MOVE SEAMLESSLY between the two
```

## 7. Glossary of Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Private cloud** | A pool of common IT resources, optimized for an organization's own use, sitting behind an on-premises firewall. |
| **Private partner cloud** | A privately owned/managed cloud built by a company that effectively becomes a cloud provider to its own business partners/customers. |
| **Indemnification** | A contractual commitment by a vendor to protect/compensate a customer against lawsuits related to service use. |
| **Appliance (private cloud)** | Custom-built hardware re-creating public cloud functionality, deployed on-premises behind the firewall. |
| **AWS Outposts** | AWS's offering providing on-premises compute/storage hardware, fully installed and maintained by AWS itself. |
| **Commercial private cloud** | A vendor-operated private cloud offered under a formal contract, for companies that don't want to build their own. |
| **Self-service portal** | An interface allowing customers of a commercial private cloud to add/subtract services on demand. |
| **Mirrored site** | A duplicate data center that can take over supporting customers if the primary data center goes offline. |
| **Cloud in a Box** | A physical cloud computing appliance that can be plugged into data center infrastructure and quickly start working, inheriting the vendor's public cloud services. |

| Type | Who Builds/Operates It | Who Manages Ongoing Operations |
|---|---|---|
| Privately owned and managed | The company itself | The company itself (becomes its own cloud provider) |
| Appliance-based | Public cloud vendor (or its hardware partner) | Vendor (fully) or shared between vendor/hardware partner |

### 8.2 Private Cloud vs. Data Center vs. Public Cloud

| | Data Center | Private Cloud | Public Cloud |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | Collection of physical machines | Pool of COMMON resources, optimized for the org | Broad, multi-tenant service offering |
| Compliance standard | N/A / company-set | Company's own security/governance/compliance | Provider's generic standard |
| Automation level | Varies | Highly automated (when mature) | Highly automated |

| | AWS Outposts | GCP + HPE |
|---|---|---|
| Hardware provider | AWS itself | Customer's choice of hardware vendor (e.g., HPE) |

## 9. Big-Picture Takeaways

1. **A private cloud is fundamentally different from "just a data center" because it's a pooled, optimized resource matched to an organization's own workflow** — the automation and resource-pooling is what makes it a "cloud" rather than merely a collection of owned servers.
2. **The three types of private clouds represent three different points on a build-vs-buy spectrum**: fully self-built and self-operated (privately owned and managed), a hybrid where the vendor supplies the technology but it lives on-premises (appliance-based), and fully outsourced to a vendor under contract (commercial private cloud).
3. **The financial services company example reveals a broader pattern**: businesses that already function like IT vendors to their own ecosystem (banks, partners, franchisees) are naturally positioned to build private partner clouds economically, since they already have the scale and expertise to make it cost-effective.
4. **Appliance-based private clouds solve a genuine tension between familiarity and compliance** — letting technical teams keep using the public-cloud tools and workflows they already know, while satisfying security/regulatory requirements that demand certain workloads stay on-premises and behind the firewall.
5. **The AWS Outposts vs. GCP+HPE comparison illustrates two different vendor philosophies for the same appliance concept**: full vendor control and simplicity (AWS) versus customer choice and negotiated flexibility (GCP's hardware-partner model).
6. **Commercial private clouds exist specifically for companies that want private-cloud-level security and governance without the burden of building it themselves** — and the shift from historically expert-dependent deployment to today's largely automated deployment mirrors the broader maturation trajectory seen across cloud technology generally.
7. **"Cloud in a Box" represents the logical endpoint of the appliance concept**: a physical, pluggable device that inherits a vendor's full public cloud service catalog, enabling truly seamless movement of applications and practices between a company's private appliance and the vendor's public cloud — collapsing much of the traditional distinction between "public" and "private" cloud at the level of day-to-day developer experience.
