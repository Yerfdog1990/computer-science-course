# Job Sequencing with Deadlines 

## 1. What Is the Problem?

You're given **n jobs**, each with a **profit** and a **deadline**. There's only **one machine**, and it can process **only one job at a time** — each job takes exactly **1 unit of time** to finish (e.g., 1 hour).

- You **earn the profit** for a job **only if it finishes by (or before) its deadline**.
- If a job can't be scheduled within its deadline, you simply **skip it** — there's no partial credit.

**Goal:** Choose a subset of jobs — and an order to run them in — such that every selected job meets its deadline **and** the total profit earned is **maximized**.

This is a classic **optimization / maximization problem**, which is why the **greedy method** applies.

### Key Terms (Recap)

- **Feasible solution:** any subset of jobs where each job in the subset can be completed by its own deadline.
- **Optimal solution:** the feasible solution that yields the **maximum total profit**.

### Core Rules & Assumptions

- **Single machine:** only one job can execute at any given time.
- **Unit time:** every job takes exactly 1 time unit.
- **Discrete slots:** slot `k` formally represents the time interval `(k-1, k]` — e.g., slot 1 = `(0,1]`, slot 2 = `(1,2]`, and so on. A job with deadline `d` can occupy any slot from 1 up to `d`.
- **All-or-nothing reward:** you earn the *full* profit if the job is completed by its deadline; otherwise you earn **zero** — there's no partial credit for late or unfinished jobs.

---

## 2. Why Greedy Works Here

Since every job takes the same fixed amount of time (1 unit), the only real decision is: **which jobs to include, and in which slot to place each one**. The greedy insight is simple:

> Always consider jobs in **decreasing order of profit** — grab the most profitable jobs first — and place each one into the **latest available time slot at or before its deadline**.

**Why the *latest* available slot, not the earliest?** Placing a job as late as its deadline allows it *leaves earlier slots open* for other jobs whose deadlines are more restrictive. This maximizes the chance that later (lower-profit) jobs can still be squeezed in somewhere.

### 2.1 Why This Is Provably Optimal

Job Sequencing with Deadlines satisfies both conditions needed for a greedy strategy to guarantee a true optimum (see the general Greedy Algorithm notes for the full definitions):

- **Greedy Choice Property:** Always picking the highest-profit remaining job — and placing it in the latest feasible slot — never blocks a better overall outcome. Locally optimal choices at each step do lead to a globally optimal schedule here.
- **Optimal Substructure:** Once a job is placed, the optimal way to schedule the *remaining* jobs in the *remaining* slots is itself a smaller instance of the same problem — so solving each remaining piece optimally builds up to the overall optimal solution.

Because both properties hold, this greedy approach isn't just a fast heuristic here — it's mathematically guaranteed to find the maximum-profit schedule.

---

## 3. Step-by-Step Algorithm

1. **Find the maximum deadline**, `D_max`, among all jobs. This tells you how many time slots exist in total (slot 1 through `D_max`).
2. **Sort jobs in descending order of profit.**
3. For each job (in that sorted order):
    - Look for a **free slot**, starting from `min(D_max, job's deadline)` and moving *backward* toward slot 1.
    - If a free slot is found, assign the job to it (mark that slot occupied) and add its profit to the running total.
    - If no free slot is available up to its deadline, **reject** the job — it's left out of the schedule.
4. Once all jobs have been considered, the occupied slots (in time order) give the final job sequence, and the running total is the maximum achievable profit.

**Pseudocode form:**

```
JobSequencing(jobs, n):
    Dmax = max(deadline[i] for i in 1..n)
    slot = array of size Dmax, all empty
    sort jobs in descending order of profit
    totalProfit = 0
    for each job in sorted jobs:
        for t = min(Dmax, job.deadline) down to 1:
            if slot[t] is empty:
                slot[t] = job
                totalProfit += job.profit
                break     // move to next job
    return slot, totalProfit
```

## 4. Worked Example 1 (5 jobs, max deadline = 3)

| Job | Profit | Deadline |
|---|---|---|
| T1 | 20 | 2 |
| T2 | 15 | 2 |
| T3 | 10 | 1 |
| T4 | 5 | 3 |
| T5 | 1 | 3 |

**Max deadline = 3**, so there are 3 slots: `[1] [2] [3]`.

Processing jobs in descending order of profit:

- **T1** (profit 20, deadline 2): latest free slot ≤ 2 is **slot 2** → assign T1 there.
- **T2** (profit 15, deadline 2): slot 2 is taken, so try slot 1 → **slot 1** is free → assign T2 there.
- **T3** (profit 10, deadline 1): only slot 1 qualifies, but it's occupied → **rejected**.
- **T4** (profit 5, deadline 3): slot 3 is free → assign T4 there.
- **T5** (profit 1, deadline 3): slot 3 is now taken, and no earlier slot qualifies (deadline is 3, but 1 and 2 are full) → **rejected**.

**Final schedule:** Slot 1 = T2, Slot 2 = T1, Slot 3 = T4 → sequence **T2 → T1 → T4**
**Total profit:** 15 + 20 + 5 = **40**

### 4.1 Same Example, as a Running Cumulative Table

This problem is often tracked using **time-interval slot notation** instead of plain slot numbers — e.g., slot 2 is written as the interval `[1,2]` (meaning "the period from hour 1 to hour 2"), and slot 1 is `[0,1]`. Each row also shows the **solution accumulated so far** and the **running total profit**, which is a useful way to trace the algorithm step by step:

| Job Considered | Slot Assigned | Solution So Far | Cumulative Profit |
|---|---|---|---|
| — | — | — | 0 |
| J1 (deadline 2) | `[1,2]` | {J1} | 20 |
| J2 (deadline 2) | `[1,2]` taken → try `[0,1]` → assigned | {J1, J2} | 20 + 15 = 35 |
| J3 (deadline 1) ✗ | `[0,1]` and `[1,2]` both taken → **rejected** | {J1, J2} | 35 (unchanged) |
| J4 (deadline 3) | `[0,1]`, `[1,2]` taken → try `[2,3]` → assigned | {J1, J2, J4} | 35 + 5 = 40 |
| J5 (deadline 3) ✗ | all slots `[0,1] [1,2] [2,3]` taken → **rejected** | {J1, J2, J4} | **40** |

**Final result:** the same schedule as above — J1, J2, and J4 are completed (in the order J2 → J1 → J4, since J1 and J2 share slots that can be arranged either way), for a total profit of **40**. J3 and J5 are dropped since no slot remained within their deadlines.

## 5. Worked Example 2 (n = 7 jobs, max deadline = 4)

| Job | J1 | J2 | J3 | J4 | J5 | J6 | J7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Profit | 35 | 30 | 25 | 20 | 15 | 12 | 5 |
| Deadline | 3 | 4 | 4 | 2 | 3 | 1 | 2 |

**Max deadline = 4**, so there are 4 slots: `[1] [2] [3] [4]`.

Processing jobs in descending order of profit (they're already sorted: J1 → J2 → J3 → J4 → J5 → J6 → J7):

- **J1** (profit 35, deadline 3): latest free slot ≤ 3 is **slot 3** → assign.
- **J2** (profit 30, deadline 4): latest free slot ≤ 4 is **slot 4** → assign.
- **J3** (profit 25, deadline 4): slot 4 is taken; next check slot 3 — also taken → fall back to **slot 2** → assign.
- **J4** (profit 20, deadline 2): slot 2 is now taken → fall back to **slot 1** → assign.
- **J5** (profit 15, deadline 3): slots 1, 2, and 3 are all taken → **rejected**.
- **J6** (profit 12, deadline 1): slot 1 is taken → **rejected**.
- **J7** (profit 5, deadline 2): slots 1 and 2 are taken → **rejected**.

**Final schedule:** Slot 1 = J4, Slot 2 = J3, Slot 3 = J1, Slot 4 = J2 → sequence **J4 → J3 → J1 → J2**
**Total profit:** 20 + 25 + 35 + 30 = **110**

### 5.1 Same Example, in Interval-Slot Notation

Written the way it often appears on a timeline (each slot as a `[start, end]` interval, with profits underneath):

```
[0,1]   [1,2]   [2,3]   [3,4]
  J4      J3      J1      J2
  20      25      35      30      = 110
```

This is exactly the schedule found above — just displayed as a timeline instead of a table. J5, J6, and J7 don't appear because every slot up to their deadlines was already filled by higher-profit jobs.

## 6. Worked Example 3 (Standard 5-Job Reference Example)

This is a commonly used textbook example, useful for cross-checking your understanding:

| Job | J1 | J2 | J3 | J4 | J5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deadline | 2 | 2 | 1 | 3 | 4 |
| Profit | 20 | 60 | 40 | 100 | 80 |

**Max deadline = 4** → 4 slots available: `[1] [2] [3] [4]`

Sorted by profit (descending): **J4 (100) → J5 (80) → J2 (60) → J3 (40) → J1 (20)**

| Job Considered | Deadline | Profit | Action | Slots After |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| J4 | 3 | 100 | Latest free slot ≤ 3 → slot 3 | `[_, _, J4, _]` |
| J5 | 4 | 80 | Latest free slot ≤ 4 → slot 4 | `[_, _, J4, J5]` |
| J2 | 2 | 60 | Latest free slot ≤ 2 → slot 2 | `[_, J2, J4, J5]` |
| J3 | 1 | 40 | Latest free slot ≤ 1 → slot 1 | `[J3, J2, J4, J5]` |
| J1 | 2 | 20 | Slots 1 and 2 both full → **rejected** | `[J3, J2, J4, J5]` |

**Final schedule:** J3 → J2 → J4 → J5
**Maximum profit:** 40 + 60 + 100 + 80 = **280**

## 7. Worked Example 4 — Handling Tied Profits (Freelancer's Weekly Schedule)

This example illustrates two situations the earlier ones didn't: **two jobs with equal profit**, and a **low-profit job successfully finding a free slot late in the process**.

| Job | Profit | Deadline |
|---|---|---|
| J1 | $35 | 3 |
| J2 | $27 | 1 |
| J3 | $27 | 1 |
| J4 | $25 | 1 |
| J5 | $1 | 3 |

**Max deadline = 3**, so there are 3 slots available.

Sorted by profit (descending) — note J2 and J3 are tied at $27, so either can be processed first without changing the final profit:

| Job Considered | Deadline | Action | Running Profit |
|---|---|---|---|
| J1 ($35) | 3 | Latest free slot ≤ 3 → **Slot 3** → assigned | 35 |
| J2 ($27) | 1 | Latest free slot ≤ 1 → **Slot 1** → assigned | 35 + 27 = 62 |
| J3 ($27) | 1 | Slot 1 already taken, no other slot ≤ 1 available → **rejected** | 62 |
| J4 ($25) | 1 | Slot 1 already taken → **rejected** | 62 |
| J5 ($1) | 3 | Slots 1 and 3 taken; slot 2 is still free (≤ deadline 3) → **Slot 2** → assigned | 62 + 1 = 63 |

**Final schedule:** Slot 1 → J2 ($27), Slot 2 → J5 ($1), Slot 3 → J1 ($35)
**Total profit: $63**

**Key takeaways from this example:**
- When two jobs tie on profit (J2 and J3), it doesn't matter which is considered first — only one of them can fit in the single slot ≤ deadline 1, so the total profit is the same either way.
- Even a very low-profit job (J5, profit $1) can still get scheduled if a slot happens to be free within its deadline — greedy doesn't skip low-value jobs on principle, it simply processes every job in profit order and takes any slot that's still open.

## 8. Java Implementation

```java
import java.util.ArrayList;
import java.util.Collections;

public class JobSequencing {

    // Represents a single job with an id, deadline, and profit
    static class Job {
        String id;
        int deadline;
        int profit;

        Job(String id, int deadline, int profit) {
            this.id = id;
            this.deadline = deadline;
            this.profit = profit;
        }
    }

    static void scheduleJobs(ArrayList<Job> jobs) {
        int n = jobs.size();

        // Step 1: find the maximum deadline -> total number of time slots
        int maxDeadline = 0;
        for (Job job : jobs) {
            maxDeadline = Math.max(maxDeadline, job.deadline);
        }

        // Step 2: sort jobs in descending order of profit
        Collections.sort(jobs, (a, b) -> b.profit - a.profit);

        // Step 3: allocate jobs to the latest available slot at/before their deadline
        boolean[] slotOccupied = new boolean[maxDeadline];
        String[] result = new String[maxDeadline];
        int totalProfit = 0;

        for (Job job : jobs) {
            // Search backward from the job's deadline (capped at maxDeadline) down to slot 1
            for (int t = Math.min(maxDeadline, job.deadline) - 1; t >= 0; t--) {
                if (!slotOccupied[t]) {
                    slotOccupied[t] = true;
                    result[t] = job.id;
                    totalProfit += job.profit;
                    break; // move on to the next job
                }
            }
            // If no free slot was found, the job is simply skipped (rejected)
        }

        // Step 4: print the final schedule
        System.out.println("Scheduled sequence:");
        for (int t = 0; t < maxDeadline; t++) {
            if (slotOccupied[t]) {
                System.out.println("Slot " + (t + 1) + ": " + result[t]);
            }
        }
        System.out.println("Total Maximum Profit: " + totalProfit);
    }

    public static void main(String[] args) {
        // Example from Section 6: J1..J5 with deadlines and profits
        ArrayList<Job> jobs = new ArrayList<>();
        jobs.add(new Job("J1", 2, 20));
        jobs.add(new Job("J2", 2, 60));
        jobs.add(new Job("J3", 1, 40));
        jobs.add(new Job("J4", 3, 100));
        jobs.add(new Job("J5", 4, 80));

        scheduleJobs(jobs);
        // Expected output: J3 -> J2 -> J4 -> J5, Total Maximum Profit: 280
    }
}
```

**Sample Output:**
```
Scheduled sequence:
Slot 1: J3
Slot 2: J2
Slot 3: J4
Slot 4: J5
Total Maximum Profit: 280
```

## 9. Complexity Analysis

| Step | Cost |
|---|---|
| Sorting jobs by profit | O(n log n) |
| Slot allocation (nested loop, worst case each job scans back through all slots) | O(n²) worst case |
| **Total** | **O(n²)** worst case |
| Space (slot array + result array) | O(n) |

**Optimization note:** The O(n²) slot-search step can be improved to **O(n log n)** overall using a **Disjoint Set / Union-Find** structure (to jump directly to the nearest free slot) or a **Min-Heap**-based approach — useful when `n` is large.

## 10. Common Mistakes & How to Avoid Them

| # | Mistake | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| 10.1 | **Confusing deadline with duration** — assuming a job with deadline 3 takes 3 time units to run | Every job takes exactly **1 unit**, regardless of its deadline. The deadline just marks the **latest slot** the job may occupy. |
| 10.2 | **Filling slots left-to-right** — assigning each job to the *earliest* free slot | Always assign to the **latest** free slot ≤ the deadline. This preserves earlier slots for jobs with tighter deadlines that come later in the profit ordering. |
| 10.3 | **Sorting by deadline instead of profit** — processing jobs in deadline order | Sort by **profit, descending**. The greedy method's correctness depends specifically on this ordering — deadline order does not guarantee maximum profit. |
| 10.4 | **Off-by-one errors with slot indexing** — treating slot 0 as valid when deadlines start at 1 | Slots are **1-indexed**. Slot `k` represents the interval `(k-1, k]`; the minimum valid slot is 1. |
| 10.5 | **Skipping feasibility checks** — assuming the output schedule is automatically valid | After running the algorithm, verify that each assigned job actually sits within its own deadline — a quick sanity check catches implementation bugs. |

## 11. Real-World Applications

Beyond textbook problems, this scheduling pattern — one shared resource, unit-time tasks, deadline-bound rewards — shows up in several real systems:

| Domain | How It Applies |
|---|---|
| **OS Process Scheduling** | A CPU assigns real-time tasks to time slots, where each task has a hard deadline it must finish by. |
| **Network Packet Routing** | Routers prioritize and schedule packets according to profit-like priorities under latency SLAs (service-level agreements). |
| **Manufacturing Lines** | Single-machine job shops schedule high-value orders to minimize tardiness costs. |
| **Financial Instruments** | Option contracts and settlement windows can be modeled as deadline–profit tradeoffs. |
| **Cloud Compute Credits** | Cloud providers schedule batch workloads on limited compute nodes to maximize billed compute within time windows. |
| **Medical Resource Booking** | Operating theatres and MRI machine slots are assigned to maximize patient benefit within scheduling constraints. |

## 12. Quick Recap

- Only **one job runs at a time**, each taking exactly **1 unit of time**, in a slot formally defined as the interval `(k-1, k]`.
- A job earns its profit **only if scheduled by its deadline** — otherwise it's dropped entirely (no partial credit).
- **Greedy rule:** sort by profit (highest first), and place each job in the **latest free slot** at or before its deadline.
- This greedy strategy is **provably** optimal for this problem — it satisfies both the Greedy Choice Property and Optimal Substructure.
- Time complexity is **O(n²)** in the straightforward implementation, improvable to **O(n log n)** with Union-Find or a heap.
- Watch out for the common mistakes: confusing deadline with duration, filling slots left-to-right instead of latest-first, sorting by deadline instead of profit, and off-by-one slot indexing.
- The same pattern — one resource, unit tasks, deadline-bound profits — recurs across OS scheduling, network routing, manufacturing, finance, cloud computing, and medical resource booking.
