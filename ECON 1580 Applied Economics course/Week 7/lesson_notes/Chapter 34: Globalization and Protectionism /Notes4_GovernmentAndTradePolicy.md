## Introduction: Levels of Trade Policy

Public policy debates about how nations should respond to globalization and international trade occur at multiple levels:

* **Globally**, through institutions such as the World Trade Organization (WTO)
* **Regionally**, through trade agreements among groups of countries
* **Nationally**, through domestic laws, regulations, and trade enforcement agencies

These levels often interact and sometimes conflict with one another.

---

## 1. The World Trade Organization (WTO)

### Origins of the WTO

The World Trade Organization was officially established in **1995**, but its roots extend back to the aftermath of the **Great Depression and World War II**. During this period, countries sought to create institutions that would promote economic cooperation and prevent future conflicts.

Several major international organizations emerged:

* **The United Nations (1945)**
* **The World Bank (1946)**, to assist poorer countries
* **The International Monetary Fund (1946)**, to manage international financial issues

A third institution—the **International Trade Organization**—was planned but never created. Instead, in **1947**, 27 countries signed the **General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT)** in Geneva, Switzerland.

---

### From GATT to the WTO

GATT served as a forum for countries to negotiate reductions in tariffs and other trade barriers. The process followed a pattern:

1. Negotiate an agreement
2. Sign the agreement
3. Pause
4. Begin negotiations again

In **1995**, GATT was formally transformed into the **World Trade Organization**, expanding its scope and institutional structure.

---

---

### The WTO as an Institution

The WTO is **not a world government**. It cannot impose policies on countries. Instead, it provides:

* A forum for negotiation
* A framework for trade rules
* A dispute settlement mechanism

As of 2021:

* WTO Secretariat staff: **625 people**
* Annual budget (2020): **$197 million**

Despite its limited size, the WTO plays a central role in coordinating global trade policy.

## 2. Regional Trading Agreements

### Types of Regional Economic Integration

Regional trade agreements vary in depth and scope, including:

* **Free trade agreements**: Remove tariffs and quotas among members
* **Common markets**: Free trade plus a common external trade policy
* **Economic unions**: Common markets plus coordinated fiscal and monetary policies

Many countries participate in both the WTO and regional agreements.

---

### The European Union (EU)

The **European Union** is the most advanced example of regional economic integration.

* Originated after World War II to reduce the risk of conflict
* Evolved from a free trade area into a common market and then a full economic union
* Introduced a common currency, the **euro**, in the early 2000s
* Promotes free movement of goods, labor, and capital

In **2016**, the United Kingdom voted to leave the EU, completing its withdrawal in **January 2020**.

---

### NAFTA and Other U.S. Agreements

For the United States, the most prominent regional agreement was the **North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA)** involving:

* Canada
* Mexico
* United States

NAFTA was officially replaced in **2020** by the **United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement (USMCA)**.

The U.S. also participates in other agreements, such as:

* The Caribbean Basin Initiative
* A free trade agreement with Israel

---

## 3. Trade Policy at the National Level

Trade policy is also enacted domestically through national laws and agencies.

### U.S. Trade Policy Institutions

In the United States:

* Import quotas exist on products like **sugar** to protect domestic producers
* The **Department of Commerce** investigates dumping
* The **U.S. International Trade Commission** determines whether domestic industries are harmed
* The president may impose tariffs if unfair trade practices are confirmed

---

### National Laws versus International Agreements

A tension often exists between:

* Domestic pressure for protectionism
* International agreements aimed at reducing trade barriers

International agreements can help governments resist domestic special-interest pressures. Politicians can point to WTO commitments as a reason not to impose new restrictions.

---

## 4. Long-Term Trends in Barriers to Trade

Despite frequent disputes and political controversy, the **long-term trend** has been toward **lower trade barriers**.

### Declining Tariffs

* Average tariffs in industrialized countries were **40% in 1946**
* By **1990**, they had fallen to **less than 5%**
* U.S. tariffs dropped to **below 2%** by the end of the twentieth century

As tariffs fell, negotiations shifted toward nontariff barriers and other trade issues.

---

### Technological Change and Trade Growth

Alongside policy changes, technological advances in:

* Transportation
* Communication
* Information management

have made international trade easier and more extensive.

---

### Recent Shifts

Two major developments in **2016** altered these trends:

* The UK’s vote to leave the EU
* The election of President Trump in the United States

In **2018**, U.S. tariffs on many Chinese imports rose by about **25%**. As of **2022**, it remains unclear whether these policies will be reversed or modified.

---

## Conclusion

Governments shape trade policy at:

* The **global level** through the WTO
* The **regional level** through trade agreements
* The **national level** through domestic laws and enforcement

Although disputes dominate headlines, the long-term pattern has been a substantial reduction in trade barriers, contributing to the expansion of global trade. Recent political developments, however, suggest that trade policy remains dynamic and contested.
