# Measuring Trade Balances

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you will be able to:

* Explain **merchandise trade balance**, **current account balance**, and **unilateral transfers**
* Identify the **components of the U.S. current account balance**
* Calculate the **merchandise trade balance** and the **current account balance** using import and export data for a country

---

## 1. Measuring International Trade

A few decades ago, economists mainly measured trade by tracking **physical goods**—the solid items that planes, trains, and trucks transported between countries. This approach focuses on **exports and imports of goods** and is known as the **merchandise trade balance**.

Economists call this measurement the **merchandise trade balance**.

However, in most **high-income economies**, including the United States:

* **Goods** account for **less than half** of total production
* **Services** account for **more than half** of total production

Over the last two decades, there has been a **surge in international trade in services**, driven by advances in **telecommunications and computers**. These technologies have made it possible to export and import services such as:

* Customer services
* Finance
* Law
* Advertising
* Management consulting
* Software
* Construction engineering
* Product design

Although most global trade still takes the form of **goods rather than services**, governments and the media continue to prominently report the **merchandise trade balance**. Economists, however, usually rely on broader measures, such as the **balance of trade** or the **current account balance**, which include additional international flows of income and foreign aid.

---

## 2. Merchandise Trade Balance vs. Current Account Balance

* **Merchandise trade balance**: Measures exports and imports of **goods only**
* **Balance of trade**: Includes exports and imports of **goods and services**
* **Current account balance**: Includes:

    * Goods
    * Services
    * Income from investments
    * Unilateral transfers (foreign aid and similar payments)

**Important:**
Do not confuse the **balance of trade** with the **current account balance**. The current account balance is broader and includes income from investment and transfers in addition to trade.

---

## 3. Components of the U.S. Current Account Balance

The U.S. current account balance has **four main components**:

### 1. Trade in Goods (Merchandise Trade Balance)

This includes exports and imports of physical goods. If imports exceed exports, the country runs a **merchandise trade deficit**.

### 2. Trade in Services

This includes exports and imports of services. Although smaller than goods trade, services trade has grown significantly. For example:

* In 2020, U.S. exports of services were about **one-half** of exports of goods
* In 1980, services exports were only **one-fifth** of goods exports

### 3. Income Receipts and Payments

This refers to income earned from **financial investments**, including:

* Income U.S. investors receive from foreign investments (money flowing into the U.S.)
* Income foreign investors receive from investments in the United States (money flowing out)

From an economic perspective, this income is treated like trade because it represents an economic transaction in financial markets.

### 4. Unilateral Transfers

These are payments made **without receiving a good or service in return**, such as:

* U.S. government economic or military aid
* Foreign donations by private charities
* Money sent abroad by individuals (e.g., remittances)

Unilateral transfers are treated like **imports** because money leaves the country. For the United States, this component is usually negative, although exceptions exist (for example, during the 1991 Gulf War).

## 5. How the U.S. Government Collects Trade Statistics

The **Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA)** within the U.S. Department of Commerce compiles trade statistics using multiple sources:

* Merchandise trade data from **monthly filings** by importers and exporters to the Census Bureau
* Surveys to measure **international trade in services**
* Surveys tracking **investment flows**
* Travel surveys for U.S. residents visiting Canada and Mexico
* Government data on **foreign aid**
* Surveys of charitable organizations making foreign donations

The BEA cross-checks data with:

* Shipping industry data
* Reports from banks and financial institutions
* U.S. Treasury records
* Trade data reported by other countries and international organizations

## 6. Calculating Trade Balances (Work It Out)

### Known Information

* Exports in goods: $1,046
* Imports in goods: $1,562
* Exports in services: $509
* Imports in services: $371
* Income received by U.S. investors abroad: $561
* Income received by foreign investors in the U.S.: $472
* Unilateral transfers: $130

### Steps Summary

1. Enter exports of goods and services
2. Enter imports of goods and services
3. Record income flowing into the U.S. under exports
4. Record income flowing out under imports
5. Enter unilateral transfers as imports (negative)
6. Subtract imports from exports to find balances
7. Merchandise balance = exports of goods − imports of goods
8. Sum all balances to find the current account balance

---

## 8. Key Takeaways

* The **merchandise trade balance** measures only goods
* The **current account balance** provides a broader picture of international transactions
* Services, income flows, and unilateral transfers significantly affect a country’s overall trade position
* A negative current account balance indicates that more money is leaving the country than entering it
