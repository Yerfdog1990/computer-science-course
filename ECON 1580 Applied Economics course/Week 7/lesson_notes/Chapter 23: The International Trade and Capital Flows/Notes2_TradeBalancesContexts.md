## 1. Understanding Trade Balances Over Time

Economists analyze trade balances using historical data to understand how a country’s international economic position changes over time. Two key measures are commonly used:

* **Current account balance**
* **Merchandise trade balance**, which measures exports and imports of **goods only**

The history of the U.S. current account balance in recent decades can be presented in multiple ways to highlight different aspects of trade performance.

## 3. Trade Balances as a Share of GDP

By dividing the trade balance by GDP for each year, this measure:

* Factors out the effects of **inflation**
* Accounts for **growth in the real economy**

The graph shows that:

* Both balances declined around **1986**
* They increased gradually until **1991**
* Both dropped again, reaching a low point around **2005**
* As of **2013**:

    * The current account balance was about **–2% of GDP**
    * The merchandise trade balance was about **–4% of GDP**

---

## 4. Historical Patterns in U.S. Trade

By either dollar amounts or percentages of GDP, a clear pattern emerges in U.S. trade history:

* **1960s–1970s**: Mostly **small trade surpluses**
* **1980s**: Trade deficits increased rapidly
* **1991**: A brief, small surplus
* **Late 1990s to mid-2000s**: Large and growing trade deficits
* **2009**: Trade deficit declined following the recession
* **2010–2019**: Partial rebound and relative stability
* **2020**: Trade deficit fell again

## 6. Comparing the United States with Other Countries

Key observations from Table 23.4 include:

* The **United States** has a current account balance of **–2.9% of GDP**
* **Germany** runs a large trade surplus of **7.0% of GDP**
* **Japan**, **Sweden**, **Korea**, and **China** also run trade surpluses
* Countries such as **Brazil**, **Canada**, **Nigeria**, and the **United Kingdom** run trade deficits
* The **world total** current account balance equals **0.0%**, since global surpluses and deficits must balance

The United States exports a smaller share of its GDP (**10.2%**) compared to many other countries, especially highly export-oriented economies such as Germany, Sweden, and Mexico.

---

## 7. Key Takeaways

* Trade balances can be measured in **dollars** or as a **percentage of GDP**
* The U.S. has shifted from small surpluses to persistent trade deficits since the 1980s
* Expressing trade balances as a share of GDP allows for better comparison across time and countries
* Compared with many other nations, the U.S. runs relatively moderate but consistent trade deficits
