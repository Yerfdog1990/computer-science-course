## The Difference between Level of Trade and the Trade Balance

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you will be able to:

* Identify three factors that influence a country's level of trade
* Differentiate between balance of trade and level of trade

---

### Level of Trade versus Balance of Trade

At first glance, a nation’s **level of trade** may sound like the same concept as its **balance of trade**, but these are actually two distinct ideas. A country can have a **high level of trade**—meaning exports and imports make up a large share of its GDP—while still having exports and imports that are nearly equal. Conversely, a country can have a **low level of trade** as a share of GDP and still experience a very large trade surplus or trade deficit.

This distinction was emphasized earlier in discussions of trade measurement, which showed that trade levels and trade balances often tell very different stories about an economy.

---

### What Is the Level of Trade?

A country’s **level of trade** indicates how much of its total production it exports to the rest of the world. It is commonly measured as:

* **Exports as a percentage of GDP**

The level of trade reflects how globalized an economy is. A high level of trade means that a significant portion of domestic production is sold abroad.

For example, Germany has a high level of trade, exporting close to 50% of its total production. In contrast, the United States exports only about 10% of its GDP, which is relatively low by world standards.

---

### What Is the Balance of Trade?

The **balance of trade** compares the value of a country’s exports to the value of its imports:

* A **trade surplus** occurs when exports exceed imports
* A **trade deficit** occurs when imports exceed exports

The balance of trade tells us whether a country is a net exporter or net importer, but it does not tell us how large trade is relative to the size of the economy.

For example, the United States has a low level of trade, but it has run very large trade deficits—over $600 billion in some years. This shows that a low level of trade does not imply a small trade imbalance.

---

### Factors That Influence a Country’s Level of Trade

Three main factors strongly influence a nation’s level of trade:

#### 1. Size of the Economy

Large economies, such as the United States and Japan, can conduct much of their economic activity internally. As a result, exports and imports tend to be a smaller share of GDP. Smaller economies, such as Sweden, cannot produce as wide a range of goods and services domestically and therefore tend to trade more, resulting in higher ratios of exports and imports to GDP.

#### 2. Geographic Location

Countries with many nearby neighbors tend to trade more because transportation and communication costs are lower. Proximity encourages cross-border exchange, increasing the level of trade.

#### 3. History and Policy toward Trade

Some countries have long-established patterns of international trade, while others have historically limited trade through policy choices. Nations with a strong tradition of openness to trade tend to have higher levels of trade than those that have sought to inhibit trade.

---

### Examples of Trade Levels across Countries

Sweden, a relatively small economy with many nearby European trading partners and a long history of foreign trade, has a high level of trade.

Brazil and India, which are large economies and have often restricted trade in recent decades, tend to have lower levels of trade.

The United States and Japan are extremely large economies with comparatively few nearby trading partners. Both have low levels of trade by world standards, with export-to-GDP ratios about half of the world average.

---

### Trade Balance Is Separate from Trade Level

The balance of trade is independent of the level of trade. Countries with similar trade levels can have very different trade balances:

* The United States has a low level of trade and ran large trade deficits from the mid-1980s into the 2000s.
* Japan has a low level of trade by world standards but has typically recorded large trade surpluses.
* Germany and the United Kingdom have medium to high levels of trade; in 2020, Germany had a moderate trade surplus while the United Kingdom had a moderate trade deficit.
* Sweden had a high level of trade and a moderate trade surplus in 2020, while Canada had a high level of trade and a moderate trade deficit in the same year.

These examples show that trade level and trade balance describe different aspects of an economy’s interaction with the global market.

---

### Why the Distinction Matters

It is entirely possible for a country with a low level of trade to experience large trade deficits. Likewise, a country with exports and imports that are nearly balanced may still have a very high level of trade relative to GDP.

A high level of trade can be beneficial because it allows countries to specialize according to comparative advantage. At the same time, policymakers may be concerned about macroeconomic instability associated with persistent trade deficits, even if they support open trade.

---

### Clear It Up: Trade Surpluses in Colonial India

During British rule from 1858 to 1947, India consistently ran trade surpluses with Great Britain. If trade surpluses were always a sign of economic strength, this would suggest that colonial India was dominating Britain—an interpretation that is clearly incorrect.

Instead, India’s trade surpluses meant that financial capital flowed from India to Great Britain each year. In India, this outflow was widely criticized as the “drain,” and ending it was one of the motivations behind the push for independence. This example shows that trade surpluses, like trade deficits, must be interpreted in context.

---

### Final Thoughts about Trade Levels and Trade Balances

Trade deficits, trade surpluses, and even a trade balance of zero can each be either positive or negative, depending on economic circumstances. The key issue is not whether a nation is borrowing or lending internationally, but whether that borrowing or lending makes sense given the country’s economic conditions.

If we described a trade deficit as “attracting foreign financial capital,” it might sound more appealing. If we described a trade surplus as “shipping financial capital abroad,” it might sound less appealing. Either way, understanding trade requires looking beyond labels and focusing on how flows of goods, services, and financial capital interact.

---

### Bring It Home: More than Meets the Eye in the Congo

Everyday economic choices—such as saving or investing in international funds—affect global flows of goods, services, and money. A trade surplus or deficit alone does not tell us whether an economy is healthy.

For example, the Democratic Republic of the Congo ran a trade surplus in 2013, yet its current account balance was –$2.8 billion. In this case, political stability and post–civil war rebuilding attracted foreign investment. The negative current account balance reflected capital inflows that supported reconstruction, illustrating once again that trade balances must be interpreted within their broader economic context.
