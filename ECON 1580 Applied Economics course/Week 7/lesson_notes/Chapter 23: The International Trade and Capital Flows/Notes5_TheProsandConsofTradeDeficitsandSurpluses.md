## The Pros and Cons of Trade Deficits and Surpluses

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you will be able to:

* Identify three ways in which borrowing money or running a trade deficit can result in a healthy economy
* Identify three ways in which borrowing money or running a trade deficit can result in a weaker economy

---

### Trade Balances and Financial Capital Flows

Because flows of international trade always involve flows of financial payments, trade balances are directly linked to international flows of financial capital. In economic terms, asking whether a trade deficit or a trade surplus is “good” or “bad” is the same as asking whether it is beneficial for an economy to rely on **net inflows of foreign financial capital** or to make **net investments abroad**.

Conventional wisdom often suggests that borrowing is irresponsible and that a prudent country should rely only on its own resources. However, just as with individuals and businesses, borrowing can be either beneficial or harmful depending on **how the borrowed funds are used**. There is no economic merit in completely abstaining from international financial capital markets.

---

## When Trade Deficits Can Be Beneficial

A trade deficit means that a country is importing more goods and services than it exports, which corresponds to a **net inflow of foreign financial capital**. This can support a healthy economy under several conditions.

### 1. Borrowing to Finance Productive Investment

It makes economic sense to borrow when the funds are used for investments that generate long-term payoffs. For individuals, borrowing for education can raise future earnings enough to repay the loan and still be better off. For businesses, borrowing to buy machinery is sensible if the resulting increase in output and profits exceeds the cost of the loan.

Similarly, a country can benefit from a trade deficit if it borrows from abroad and invests wisely in projects that raise productivity and long-term economic growth. If these investments succeed, the country can repay foreign investors over time and still enjoy a higher standard of living.

### 2. Historical Example: The United States in the 19th Century

The United States provides a vivid historical example. From 1831 to 1875, the U.S. ran trade deficits in 40 out of 45 years, meaning it consistently imported capital from abroad. Much of this foreign capital was invested in railroads and public infrastructure such as roads, water systems, and schools.

These investments generated substantial economic payoffs and helped fuel rapid economic growth. By around 1900, the U.S. had achieved the highest per capita GDP in the world. Although foreign capital accounted for only about 6–10% of total investment funds in most years, it was clearly a help rather than a hindrance.

### 3. Modern Example: South Korea

South Korea ran trade deficits during much of the 1970s and imported capital from abroad. At the same time, it maintained very high rates of investment in factories, equipment, and infrastructure. As a result, its economy grew rapidly.

From the mid-1980s to the mid-1990s, South Korea often ran trade surpluses, effectively repaying its earlier borrowing by sending capital abroad. This pattern illustrates how trade deficits can support growth in earlier stages of development and be followed by surpluses later on.

## When Trade Deficits Can Be Harmful

While trade deficits are not inherently bad, they can weaken an economy under certain circumstances.

### 1. Borrowing Without Productive Investment

One major risk arises when borrowed funds are not invested in ways that raise productivity. Several large Latin American economies, including Mexico and Brazil, ran large trade deficits and borrowed heavily from abroad in the 1970s. However, these inflows of foreign capital did not sufficiently boost productivity.

When global economic conditions changed in the 1980s, these countries faced severe difficulties repaying their debts. Similarly, a number of African nations borrowed heavily during the 1970s and 1980s but failed to invest in productive economic assets. The result was large interest payments with little or no economic growth to show for the borrowed funds.

### 2. Sudden Reversals of Capital Flows

A second danger is the risk that foreign capital may flow in quickly and then suddenly flow out again. In the mid-1990s, several East Asian economies—including Thailand, Indonesia, Malaysia, and South Korea—ran large trade deficits and imported capital from abroad.

In 1997–1998, foreign investors abruptly lost confidence and pulled their money out of these economies. The rapid outflow of capital destabilized banks, collapsed asset prices, and plunged these countries into deep recessions. This experience highlights how reliance on foreign capital can increase vulnerability to financial crises.

### 3. Size and Persistence of Deficits

Large and persistent trade deficits can be a legitimate cause for concern, especially if they are accompanied by sustained foreign borrowing. Although a very large economy like the United States is less likely to be destabilized than smaller economies, prolonged current account deficits can still expose an economy to financial shocks. Policymakers should therefore pay close attention to historical cases where extensive foreign borrowing ended badly.

## Are Trade Surpluses Always Beneficial?

Just as trade deficits are not always harmful, trade surpluses are not guaranteed to produce strong economic performance.

### Example: Germany and Japan

Germany and Japan have run substantial trade surpluses for much of the past three decades. Despite these surpluses, both countries have experienced recessions, and neither has enjoyed especially robust long-term growth in recent years. This demonstrates that a persistent trade surplus is not a guarantee of economic health.

### Japan Since the 1990s

Japan is particularly well known for its trade surpluses, which have often approached $100 billion per year since 1990. During Japan’s rapid growth in the 1960s and 1970s, many observers viewed its trade surplus as a sign of economic strength.

However, since 1990, Japan’s economy has grown slowly, with real GDP growth averaging about 1% per year and unemployment gradually rising. Japan’s trade surplus reflects very high domestic saving relative to domestic investment, rather than strong economic growth. Slow growth and relatively low consumption of imports have kept exports higher than imports. Although Japan briefly ran a trade deficit in 2013 due to high oil import costs, it returned to surplus by 2015.

---

## Summary: Weighing the Pros and Cons

Trade deficits and surpluses should be understood as outcomes of international financial capital flows, not as automatic indicators of economic success or failure.

* **Trade deficits can support a healthy economy** when foreign capital is invested in productive assets that raise long-term growth.
* **Trade deficits can weaken an economy** when borrowed funds are poorly invested or when sudden capital outflows trigger financial crises.
* **Trade surpluses are not a guarantee of economic strength**, as they may reflect weak domestic investment or slow consumption rather than robust growth.
