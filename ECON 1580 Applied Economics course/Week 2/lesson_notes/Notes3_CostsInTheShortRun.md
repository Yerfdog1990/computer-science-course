# **Lesson Notes: Explicit Costs, Implicit Costs, and Accounting vs. Economic Profit**

## **Learning Objectives**

By the end of this section, you will be able to:

* Clearly distinguish between **explicit costs** and **implicit costs**
* Explain why **implicit costs matter for economic decision-making**
* Understand the relationship between **revenue, total cost, and profit**
* Differentiate between **accounting profit** and **economic profit**
* Apply concepts using **realistic business examples**

---

# **1. Profit: The Core Objective**

Every firm—small or large—seeks to earn **profit**, which is formally defined as:

> Profit = Total Revenue (TR) - Total Cost (TC)

### **Total Revenue (TR)**

Income from selling goods or services:

> TR = P \times Q

* **Price (P)** = price per unit
* **Quantity (Q)** = number of units sold

Revenue depends on **consumer demand**, which we will study in depth later.

---

# **2. Total Cost: The Sum of All Input Costs**

Production requires inputs: land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship.
Each has a cost.

Economists categorize these costs into **explicit** and **implicit**.

---

# **3. Explicit Costs (Out-of-Pocket Costs)**

**Definition:**
Explicit costs are **direct, monetary payments** a firm makes for inputs.

Examples:

* Salaries and wages paid to employees
* Rent for office or factory space
* Raw material costs
* Utility bills
* Advertising expenses
* Software subscription fees

These appear clearly on financial statements.

### **Practical Example**

A bakery pays:

* \$4,000/month for flour, sugar, etc.
* \$2,500/month for rent
* \$3,200/month in wages

Total explicit monthly cost:

> 4,000 + 2,500 + 3,200 = 9,700

This is the cost an accountant would record.

---

# **4. Implicit Costs (Opportunity Costs of Owned Resources)**

**Definition:**
Implicit costs represent the **value of resources the firm already owns and uses**, without any direct payment being made.
They are **opportunity costs**—the income the owner *forgoes* by using resources in the current business instead of their next-best alternative.

Common implicit cost examples:

* The salary the owner could earn elsewhere
* Use of the owner’s home as an office
* Using personal savings to fund the business (forgoing interest income)
* Using a building you own instead of renting it out
* Loss of leisure time when the owner works more hours

### **Practical Example**

A web developer quits a job paying \$70,000/year to run a startup.

>Even if she doesn’t pay herself a salary, the **implicit cost** is:
>
> $70,000 -> forgone salary

Economists *must* include this cost to determine whether her business is truly worthwhile.

---

# **5. Why Implicit Costs Matter**

**Accounting** focuses on money that changes hands.
**Economics** focuses on **choices and trade-offs**.

Implicit costs help answer economic questions like:

* “Is the business truly the best use of my resources?”
* “Would I be better off doing something else?”

A business can be **accounting profitable** but **economically unprofitable**.

---

# **6. Accounting Profit vs. Economic Profit**

## **Accounting Profit**

> Accounting Profit = TR - Explicit Costs

This is the profit used for:

* Taxes
* Reporting to investors
* Financial statements

## **Economic Profit**

> Economic Profit = TR - (Explicit Costs + Implicit Costs)

Economic profit is the **true profitability** because it includes *opportunity costs*.

---

# **7. Why This Difference Matters**

* If **economic profit = 0**, the firm is earning a **normal profit**.

    * This means the owner is doing just as well as their next-best alternative.
* If **economic profit > 0**, the business is better than alternatives.
* If **economic profit < 0**, the owner could earn more by switching to another option.

---

# **8. Work It Out Example: Eryn’s Legal Practice**

### Step 1: Explicit Costs

* Office rent: $50,000
* Clerk salary: $35,000

> Total explicit costs = 50,000 + 35,000 = 85,000

### Step 2: Accounting Profit

Revenue: $200,000

> 200,000 - 85,000 = 115,000

→ **Accounting profit = $115,000**

### Step 3: Implicit Cost

Eryn currently earns $125,000 at her law firm.

This is her **forgone salary** → implicit cost = \$125,000

### Step 4: Economic Profit

> 200,000 - 85,000 - 125,000 = -10,000

→ **Economic profit = –$10,000**

**Interpretation:**
Eryn would earn $10,000 more if she stayed at her corporate job.

---

# **9. Additional Real-World Examples**

## **Example A: Coffee Shop Owner**

Maria uses $100,000 of her savings to open a coffee shop.
She forgoes 5% interest she could earn in a bank account.

Implicit cost = 0.05 × 100,000 = **\$5,000** per year in forgone interest.

She also works full-time but pays herself \$0.
Her alternative job pays \$30/hour → 40 hours/week → \$62,400/year.

> Total implicit cost = 5,000 + 62,400 = 67,400

Even if the shop shows an **accounting profit of $50,000**, her:

>Economic profit = 50,000 - 67,400 = -17,400

Economically, she is worse off.

---

## **Example B: Using Your Own Building**

A family owns a building they use as a restaurant.
If rented out, it would earn $40,000/year.

That \$40,000 is an **implicit cost** of operating the restaurant.

---

## **Example C: Tech Freelancer**

Ali quits a job paying $55,000/year to become a freelance programmer.

He earns:

* Revenue: \$90,000
* Explicit costs (equipment + software subscriptions): \$10,000

> Accounting profit: 90,000 - 10,000 = 80,000

> Economic profit: 90,000 - 10,000 - 55,000 = 25,000

Ali is **economically better off** freelancing.

---

# **10. Summary Table**

| Concept               | Includes                                      | Examples                                                  |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Explicit Costs**    | Monetary payments                             | Rent, wages, raw materials                                |
| **Implicit Costs**    | Opportunity costs of owner-provided resources | Forgone salary, forgone rent, lost interest, lost leisure |
| **Accounting Profit** | TR – explicit costs                           | Profit reported for taxes                                 |
| **Economic Profit**   | TR – (explicit + implicit costs)              | True economic success                                     |

---

# **11. Key Takeaways**

* **Explicit costs** are paid out-of-pocket; **implicit costs** reflect opportunities given up.
* **Accounting profit** can be positive even when **economic profit** is negative.
* Economists care about **economic profit** because it tells whether resources are being used in their most valuable way.
* A business earning **zero economic profit** is still viable—it means the owner is earning a **normal return**.
* Implicit costs often matter most in **small businesses and entrepreneurship**, where owners invest their own time and resources.
