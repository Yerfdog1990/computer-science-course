# **Lesson Notes: Explicit & Implicit Costs, Accounting Profit & Economic Profit**

## **1. Big Idea: What Is Profit?**

Every firm tries to earn **profit**, which is the difference between the money coming in and the money going out:

>Profit = Total Revenue - Total Cost

### **Total Revenue (TR)**

>Revenue = Price × Quantity sold
> 
>Example: If a café sells 200 coffees at \$3 each → TR = 200 × 3 = \$600

### **Total Cost (TC)**

Total cost includes *all* costs of using resources to produce goods or services.

---

## **2. Explicit vs. Implicit Costs**

Economics separates costs into two kinds:

### **A. Explicit Costs**

These are **actual cash payments**.
Examples:

* Wage payments
* Rent
* Utilities
* Payments for raw materials

These appear on financial statements.

### **B. Implicit Costs**

These are **opportunity costs** — what the firm *gives up* by using resources it already owns.

Examples:

* The owner working without taking a salary
* Using your own building instead of renting it out
* Using your savings to fund a business instead of earning interest
* Giving up a job elsewhere

These do **not** appear on accounting statements, but they matter for decision-making.

## **3. Why These Costs Matter: Two Types of Profit**

A business can measure success in two ways:

### **A. Accounting Profit**

>Accounting Profit = Total Revenue - Explicit Costs

This is the profit reported on financial statements and for tax purposes.
**Does NOT include implicit costs.**

### **B. Economic Profit**

>Economic Profit = Total Revenue - (Explicit + Implicit Costs)

This measures the *true* profitability because it includes opportunity costs.
A firm can have positive accounting profit but zero (or negative) economic profit.

---

## **4. Practical Example (Eryn’s Law Firm)**

### **Eryn’s Expected Revenue:**

$200,000 per year

### **Explicit Costs:**

>* Office rent: $50,000
>
>* Law clerk salary: $35,000
>
>**Total explicit costs = \$85,000**

**Accounting profit:**
> 200,000 - 85,000 = 115,000


So on paper, the business looks profitable.

### **Implicit Cost:**

Eryn gives up her current salary of **\$125,000**.
This is the opportunity cost of becoming self-employed.

### **Economic Profit:**

> 200,000 - 85,000 - 125,000 = -10,000


So her economic profit is **–$10,000**, meaning she is worse off than staying at her old job.

### **Extra implicit costs she might experience:**

* Lost leisure time
* Higher stress
* Unpaid overtime

---

## **5. Additional Real-World Examples**

### **Example 1: Small Coffee Shop**

* Explicit costs: beans, barista wages, rent
* Implicit costs: owner works 40 hours/week without pay; owner uses their own building

If the owner could rent the building to someone else for $2,000/month, that forgone rent is an **implicit cost**.

### **Example 2: A freelancer using savings**

If someone invests \$20,000 of personal savings into starting a business instead of keeping it in an account earning 5% interest, the lost \$1,000 per year is an **implicit cost**.

---

## **6. Why Economic Profit Matters**

* A company with **zero economic profit** is still viable — it simply covers all opportunity costs.
* A firm with **positive economic profit** earns more than its next-best alternative use of resources.
* A firm with **negative economic profit** might still operate in the short run, but long-run survival is questionable.
